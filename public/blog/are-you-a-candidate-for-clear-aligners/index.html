Are You a Candidate for Clear Aligners? What a Dentist Looks For

Clear aligners suit a lot of smiles — but not every one. Knowing what tips the scales toward "good fit" versus "better handled another way" helps you walk into a consultation with realistic expectations.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

Most adults with mild to moderate crowding, spacing or a bit of relapse after old orthodontic work are reasonable candidates for clear aligners — but the honest answer for any individual is "it depends on an exam." Candidacy is a clinical judgment that balances how your teeth are positioned, how healthy your gums are, and how the plan fits your daily life. Here is what a dentist is actually weighing.

It also helps to understand what "candidate" really means here. It is not a yes-or-no label stamped on a person; it is a match between a particular set of tooth movements and a tool that can deliver them predictably. The same person might be an excellent candidate for a modest correction and a poor one for a dramatic overhaul. That is why the useful question is rarely "am I a candidate?" in the abstract, but "is this approach the right fit for the specific result I want?"

Where aligners tend to shine

Certain situations are squarely in the aligner comfort zone. Mild to moderate crowding, where teeth overlap a little; spacing or small gaps you would like to close; and relapse — teeth that have drifted years after braces because retainers were set aside. These are the cases aligners were designed to handle well, and they make up a large share of adult treatment.

Minor to moderate bite irregularities can also often be addressed, sometimes with attachments or other refinements built into the plan. The common thread is that the movements needed are within a range aligners can control predictably.

Where aligners hit their limits

There are cases where aligners are not the first choice, or need to be combined with other treatment. Severe bite discrepancies, teeth that are significantly rotated, large gaps requiring major movement, or situations needing complex root repositioning can stretch beyond what trays do reliably. In those cases a dentist may recommend fixed braces or a combined approach rather than promise an aligner-only fix.

Missing teeth and existing dental work matter too. Bridges, certain crowns and gaps change how teeth can be moved and anchored, so they shape the plan rather than simply ruling anything in or out. None of this means a case is hopeless — it means the honest recommendation might not be aligners alone.

Gum and oral health come first

This is the one that surprises people. Before any teeth are moved, the foundation they sit in has to be healthy. Active gum disease, untreated decay or significant inflammation generally need to be dealt with first. Moving teeth through unhealthy tissue can accelerate problems rather than fix anything, so a responsible plan treats and stabilizes those issues before aligners begin.

Worth Knowing

A "no, not yet" is different from a "no." Many people who aren't candidates on day one become good candidates once gum disease is controlled or decay is treated. Think of oral-health prerequisites as the first stage of treatment, not a rejection.

Age is rarely the deciding factor

People often ask whether they are "too old" for clear aligners. On its own, adult age is seldom a barrier — teeth can be moved throughout adult life because the bone-remodelling process that makes orthodontics possible continues as we age. What matters far more is the condition of your teeth, gums and bone than the number on your birth certificate.

That said, adults do bring some extra considerations to the chair: a lifetime of dental work, the odd missing tooth, gums that have receded a little, and sometimes bone that has changed around long-standing gaps. These don't rule treatment out, but they are exactly the sort of details a dentist factors into whether aligners, braces or a combination makes the most sense.

Lifestyle and discipline

Aligners ask something of you that braces do not: consistency. Because the trays only work while worn — the guidance is around 22 hours a day — a candid conversation with yourself about habits is part of the picture. Will you keep them in through busy workdays and social events? Will you remember to put them back after meals? Can you commit to cleaning teeth before reinserting?

This is not about willpower as a personality test. It is practical: a disciplined wearer gets the result the plan was built for, while an inconsistent one may stall. If you genuinely know you would struggle to keep trays in, that is useful information to share — and sometimes a reason to consider a fixed option instead.

What a candidacy conversation usually covers

If you book a consultation, expect it to be a two-way exchange rather than a sales pitch. A dentist will typically look at how your teeth are aligned and how they bite together, check the health of your gums and the bone around your roots, and review any existing dental work. Imaging or a digital scan often fills in what the eye can't see, such as the length and position of roots.

Just as importantly, a good consultation covers what aligners can and cannot do for your teeth specifically, roughly how long treatment might take, and what retention will involve afterward. If a case sits at the edge of what aligners handle well, an honest provider will say so and lay out the alternatives rather than forcing a fit.

Why there is no shortcut around the exam

Online quizzes and mail-order impressions cannot see a cavity under a filling, measure the health of the bone around your roots, or catch a bite problem that would undermine the result. A proper assessment usually includes a look at your teeth and gums, a review of your medical and dental history, and often imaging or a scan. That is what turns a general "you seem like a candidate" into a plan tailored to your mouth.

None of this is meant to diagnose you from a web page. It is to help you arrive informed. If what you have read here sounds like your situation, the sensible next step is an Invisalign assessment with a dentist who can examine your teeth and tell you, honestly, whether aligners are the right tool for your goals.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get clear aligners if I have gum disease?

Active gum disease usually needs to be treated and stabilized first. Moving teeth within unhealthy, inflamed tissue can make things worse, so a dentist typically addresses gum health before starting aligners — often clearing the way for treatment later.

Are clear aligners only for mild cases?

They are a strong fit for mild to moderate crowding, spacing and minor relapse, and can address some more involved cases. Very severe bite problems, large rotations or complex movements may be better handled by braces or a combined approach.

Does a missing tooth rule out aligners?

Not necessarily, but it changes the picture. Gaps, missing teeth and existing dental work affect how teeth can be moved and what the plan should be — precisely the kind of thing a clinical assessment sorts out.
