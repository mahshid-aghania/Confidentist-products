Bone Grafting and Implants: Why Some Patients Need It First

An implant is only as solid as the bone it sits in. When that bone has thinned, rebuilding it first isn't an upsell — it's the foundation the whole result depends on.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

If a dentist tells you you'll need a bone graft before an implant, it usually means one thing: there isn't quite enough healthy bone at the site to hold an implant securely yet. A graft rebuilds that volume so the implant has a stable foundation. It's a common, well-established step — and understanding why it's suggested makes it far less alarming.

Not everyone needs grafting. Whether you do comes down to how much bone is present and its quality, which is assessed with an examination and imaging. But the reason bone is so often in short supply traces back to something that happens quietly after any tooth is lost.

Why bone disappears after a tooth comes out

The roots of your teeth do more than hold teeth in place — they keep the surrounding bone active. Every time you chew, the force travels down the root and signals the jawbone to maintain itself. Remove the tooth, and that signal stops.

Without it, the bone in that area gradually resorbs — it shrinks in height and width. This is fastest in the months right after an extraction and continues slowly for years. A ridge that was once tall and broad can become too narrow or too short to hold a standard implant. Gum disease can accelerate the same loss, as can wearing a denture over the area for a long time.

Worth Knowing

Because bone loss begins soon after an extraction, a dentist sometimes places a small graft into the socket at the time a tooth is removed. This "socket preservation" can reduce how much bone shrinks and make a future implant more straightforward.

When grafting is needed before an implant

Grafting is considered when imaging shows the bone is too thin, too short, or too soft to anchor an implant reliably. A few common scenarios:

  • Long-standing gaps: where a tooth has been missing for years and the ridge has resorbed.
  • After gum disease: which can destroy the bone supporting teeth before they're even lost.
  • Upper back jaw: where the sinus cavity sits low and leaves little bone between it and the ridge — the classic case for a sinus lift.

In some cases a smaller graft can be done at the same appointment as implant placement. In others, the graft is placed first and given months to mature before the implant goes in. Which applies depends entirely on the individual site.

Graft types, in plain terms

"Bone graft" covers several approaches. You don't need the technical names, but it helps to know roughly where the material comes from:

  • Your own bone (autograft): taken from another site in your mouth or body. It's your own tissue, so it integrates readily.
  • Donor human bone (allograft): processed and sterilised tissue from a tissue bank, widely used and avoids a second surgical site.
  • Animal-derived bone (xenograft): usually bovine, treated to be safe and act as a scaffold your own bone grows into.
  • Synthetic material (alloplast): manufactured minerals that serve as a framework for new bone.

In every case the graft acts as scaffolding. Over time your body replaces or integrates it with your own living bone, creating the volume an implant needs.

The sinus lift, specifically

A sinus lift is a particular type of graft for the upper back jaw. The sinus is an air-filled space above the upper molars, and when teeth there are lost the sinus floor can drop closer to the ridge, leaving too little bone below. The procedure gently lifts that sinus membrane and packs graft material underneath, creating room and support for an implant. It's routine in experienced hands but, like any surgery, has a recovery period.

Healing and timelines

Grafted bone needs time to mature into solid, living bone before it can bear an implant — generally several months, though the span varies with the graft type, the location and how an individual heals. Recovery from the graft surgery itself is usually manageable, with some swelling and tenderness for a few days. Before moving to implant placement, the dentist typically takes new imaging to confirm the site is ready.

It adds time to the overall journey, but it's time spent building the foundation. An implant placed into inadequate bone is far more likely to struggle, so the graft is best seen as part of setting the implant up to succeed rather than a detour from it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do some people need a bone graft before an implant?

An implant needs enough healthy bone to anchor into. If bone has been lost after an extraction, gum disease or years of a missing tooth, a graft rebuilds the volume so the implant has a stable foundation. Whether it's needed is determined by examination and imaging.

What is a sinus lift?

It's a graft used in the upper back jaw, where the sinus cavity can sit close to the ridge. The procedure gently raises the sinus floor and adds bone beneath it so an implant can be placed with enough support.

How long is healing after a graft?

Grafted bone usually needs several months to mature before an implant can be placed, though the exact time depends on the graft type, the site and individual healing. Your dentist confirms readiness with imaging before proceeding.
