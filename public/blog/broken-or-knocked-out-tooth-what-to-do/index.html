Knocked-Out, Cracked, or Broken Tooth: What to Do in the First Hour

A tooth injury is frightening in the moment, but a few calm, correct steps in the first hour can make a real difference — sometimes the difference between saving a tooth and losing it.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 6 min read

If an adult tooth has been knocked out, the clock matters: the best chance of saving it is within about the first hour. Handle the tooth only by the crown, keep it moist — ideally in milk — and get to a dentist right away. For a cracked or broken tooth, stay calm, protect the area, save any fragments, and call for care. The steps below are safe first aid, not a substitute for seeing a professional.

First, check the bigger picture. If the injury involved a blow to the head, possible broken jaw, heavy bleeding that won't stop, or loss of consciousness, that's a medical emergency — call your local emergency number or go to a hospital before worrying about the tooth.

A knocked-out (avulsed) adult tooth

This is the most time-sensitive dental injury. If the whole tooth has come out, work quickly but gently:

  1. Pick it up by the crown — the white chewing part — never by the root. The root surface has delicate cells that help it reattach.
  2. Rinse it briefly if it's dirty, using milk or saline, or a quick pass under cool water for a second or two. Don't scrub it, don't use soap, and don't dry it or wrap it in tissue.
  3. Try to reinsert it into the socket the right way round, if you feel able. Hold it in place by biting gently on a clean cloth or gauze.
  4. If you can't reinsert it, keep it moist. The best options are cold milk, or tucked inside the cheek against the gum for an adult or older child who won't swallow it. Plain water is a poor long-term storage choice.
  5. See a dentist immediately — ideally within the hour. Bring the tooth however you're storing it.
Important

Never try to reinsert a child's baby (primary) tooth — doing so can damage the developing adult tooth beneath. For a knocked-out baby tooth, keep the area clean, control any bleeding, and call a dentist for advice. The rules above apply to permanent adult teeth.

A cracked or broken tooth

Fractures range from a tiny enamel chip to a break that exposes the inner tooth. You can't tell the depth yourself, so treat any painful or large break as needing prompt care. In the meantime:

  • Rinse your mouth gently with warm water to clean the area.
  • Save any fragments in milk or saline and bring them — a dentist can sometimes bond a piece back.
  • Use a cold compress on the outside of the cheek to limit swelling.
  • Cover a sharp edge with a piece of sugar-free dental wax or sugar-free gum to protect your tongue and cheek if it's cutting into soft tissue.
  • Take over-the-counter pain relief as directed on the package if you're sore.

A loosened or pushed-out-of-place tooth

Not every injured tooth comes all the way out. A knock can leave an adult tooth wobbly, pushed inward or outward, or driven up into the gum. These are still time-sensitive and worth prompt professional care. Don't try to force the tooth back into alignment yourself or wiggle it to test how loose it is — handling it repeatedly can do more harm. Instead, leave it where it is, avoid biting on that side, use a cold compress for comfort, and get to a dentist, who can decide whether it needs repositioning or stabilising. Gentle is the watchword with any injured tooth.

Soft-tissue cuts to the lip, gum or tongue

Mouth injuries often come with cuts to the lips, cheeks, gums or tongue, and these can bleed a lot because the area has a rich blood supply. For most minor cuts, rinsing gently and applying firm pressure with clean gauze for several minutes will slow the bleeding. A cold compress on the outside can help a swollen or split lip. A deep cut, a wound that gapes open, or bleeding that won't settle after sustained pressure may need medical attention and possibly stitches — for those, head to a doctor or hospital. If anything is embedded in the wound, don't dig it out yourself.

Controlling bleeding

Bleeding from the mouth often looks dramatic because saliva spreads it. Fold clean gauze or a clean cloth, place it over the area, and bite down with steady, firm pressure for about 10 to 15 minutes without peeking. If bleeding is heavy and doesn't slow after sustained pressure, treat it as an emergency and seek urgent medical care.

What not to do

Good intentions can cause harm. In the rush of a tooth injury, avoid these:

  • Don't touch or scrub the root of a knocked-out tooth, and don't let it dry out.
  • Don't use household glue or filler to stick a piece back — materials meant for other purposes can damage the tooth and gums.
  • Don't place an aspirin or any tablet against the gum. It doesn't relieve a toothache and can chemically burn the tissue.
  • Don't attempt any home dentistry — no drilling, filing, or digging at the tooth.
  • Don't delay because it's after hours; an avulsed adult tooth needs attention regardless of the clock.

A little readiness goes a long way

Tooth injuries happen most to children and to people playing sport, and a few habits lower both the chance and the chaos. A properly fitted mouthguard is worth its weight for anyone in a contact or ball sport, and for grinding at night. Keeping a small kit on hand — clean gauze, a travel-size container, and the knowledge that milk is a good short-term store for a knocked-out tooth — turns a panicked scramble into a few calm steps. It's also worth saving your dentist's number, including any after-hours instructions, somewhere you can reach it quickly. You can't prevent every accident, but you can decide in advance that you'll know what to do.

Getting seen quickly

Once first aid is underway, the priority is professional care. Call ahead so the clinic knows you're coming and can prepare. A practice that provides urgent dental care can assess the injury, decide whether a tooth can be stabilised or reattached, and manage any pain or infection. Keeping a few emergency essentials in mind — clean gauze, a way to store a tooth in milk — means you're ready to act if it ever happens.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best way to store a knocked-out tooth?

If you can't gently reinsert it, keep it moist in cold milk. If there's no milk, an adult or older child can hold it inside the cheek against the gum. Avoid storing it in plain water for long, and don't let it dry out. Get to a dentist as fast as you can.

Should I put an adult tooth back in myself?

For a permanent tooth, gentle reinsertion soon after the injury can help if you feel able. Hold it by the crown, rinse briefly if dirty, and place it the right way round. Never reinsert a child's baby tooth. If you can't, store it in milk and see a dentist urgently.

Can a broken tooth wait until morning?

A painless chip can often wait for a prompt appointment. A knocked-out adult tooth is time-critical — aim for care within about an hour. A break with severe pain, a large fracture, or bleeding that won't stop should be seen the same day.
