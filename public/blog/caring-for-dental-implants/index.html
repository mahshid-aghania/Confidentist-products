Caring for Dental Implants: Daily Habits and Long-Term Maintenance

An implant can't get a cavity — but the gum and bone holding it can still get sick. The daily routine that protects it is simple, and it's what keeps an implant healthy for the long run.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

A dental implant doesn't decay the way a natural tooth does — but that doesn't make it maintenance-free. The crown sits on gum and bone that very much can become inflamed if plaque builds up around it. Caring for an implant means protecting those surrounding tissues, and the daily routine for doing so is close to the one you already know.

The good news: implants are cleaned much like natural teeth. The nuance is in the spots around the implant that are easy to miss, and in staying alert to early signs of trouble so they're caught while they're still reversible.

The daily routine

Two habits carry most of the load: brushing and cleaning between teeth.

Brushing. Brush at least twice a day with a soft-bristled or electric toothbrush and a non-abrasive fluoride toothpaste, angling the bristles to clean where the crown meets the gum. This is where plaque accumulates and where inflammation starts.

Cleaning in between. This is the part people underestimate. Plaque gathers around the base of an implant just as it does around a tooth, so clean between and around it every day. Depending on your restoration, that might mean floss designed for implants, thin interdental brushes that slide into the spaces, or a water flosser. Your dental team can show you which tool reaches your particular implant best — a single-tooth crown and a full fixed arch call for different approaches.

Worth Knowing

Full-arch and bridge-style implant restorations have undersides that a regular toothbrush can't reach. These often need a water flosser or special floss threaders to clean beneath them — a step worth building into your routine from the start.

Peri-implant disease: what to watch for

Because gum and bone surround an implant, they can develop their own version of gum disease. It comes in two stages:

  • Peri-implant mucositis is inflammation of the gum around the implant — redness, puffiness or bleeding when you brush. At this stage it's usually reversible with better cleaning and professional help.
  • Peri-implantitis is the more serious stage, where inflammation has spread to the bone and begun to destroy the implant's support. It can threaten the implant if left unmanaged.

Signs worth reporting to your dentist include gums that bleed around the implant, tenderness or swelling, a bad taste, or any sense that the crown feels loose. Catching mucositis early is far easier than treating peri-implantitis later, which is the whole argument for good daily care and regular check-ups.

Professional maintenance

Home care handles the daily plaque; professional maintenance handles what builds up out of reach. Regular visits let your dental team clean around the implant with instruments suited to implant surfaces, check the gum and bone health, confirm the restoration is secure, and spot early inflammation before you'd notice it yourself. The interval between visits is set to your own risk, so follow the schedule your clinic recommends rather than stretching it out.

Risk factors to manage

Some factors make problems around an implant more likely, and they're worth being honest with your dentist about.

  • Smoking impairs healing and gum health and is associated with a higher risk of implant complications.
  • Diabetes, particularly when blood sugar isn't well controlled, can affect the tissues around an implant.
  • A history of gum disease raises the likelihood of peri-implant problems, so cleaning and recalls matter even more.
  • Grinding or clenching places heavy forces on an implant; a night guard may be suggested.

None of these automatically rules out having or keeping an implant. They simply raise the value of consistent care, and they're reasons to work closely with your dental team rather than go it alone.

The long view

Implants can serve well for many years, but that outcome is earned through upkeep rather than guaranteed by the implant itself. Treat it like a natural tooth you're determined to keep: clean it daily, clean around it thoroughly, show up for maintenance, and flag anything that changes. How well an implant lasts depends on these habits alongside your individual health — something your dentist can assess over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can implants get gum disease?

The implant itself can't decay, but the gum and bone around it can become inflamed and infected. Early, reversible inflammation is peri-implant mucositis; if it reaches the bone it's peri-implantitis. Good cleaning and professional care are the main defences.

How do I clean around an implant?

Brush twice daily as you would a natural tooth, and clean between and around the implant each day with implant floss, interdental brushes or a water flosser. Your dental team can show you which tools fit your restoration best.

Do smoking and diabetes affect implants?

Both can raise the risk of problems around an implant because they affect healing and the gum tissues. They don't automatically rule implants out, but managing them and keeping up professional maintenance becomes especially important.
