CDCP vs. Private Dental Insurance: How Coverage Compares

These two aren't competing options you choose between — they're meant for different people. Understanding the line between them clears up most of the confusion.

ConfiDentist Editorial Team • 8 min read

The most common misunderstanding about the Canadian Dental Care Plan is that it's an alternative you can weigh against a private plan. It isn't. The CDCP is specifically for Canadian residents who do not have access to private dental insurance — so for most people, only one of the two is on the table at all.

That single rule shapes almost everything else. Before comparing features, it's worth being clear on the eligibility line, because getting it wrong is where people run into trouble.

The eligibility line that separates them

The CDCP is designed to fill a gap, not to duplicate coverage you already have. Its central condition is that you don't have access to private dental insurance. "Access" is the operative word — it generally includes coverage available through:

  • an employer or a group benefits plan;
  • a pension or retiree benefits package;
  • a spouse's or family member's plan; or
  • a plan you've purchased on your own.

If you have access to any of these, you generally aren't eligible for the CDCP — even if you choose not to enrol or never submit a claim. This is a point people frequently get wrong, so it's worth confirming against the official criteria rather than assuming.

The logic behind the rule is straightforward: the CDCP is public money meant to reach people who would otherwise go without coverage, not to subsidize those who already have a private option. That's why "access" rather than "use" is the test. It can feel counterintuitive if you have a workplace plan you consider weak, but the plan's existence — not its generosity — is what the eligibility rule looks at. If you're genuinely unsure whether a benefit you have counts, that's precisely the kind of question to put to the official source rather than guess at.

Check the Source

Whether a particular benefit counts as "access to private insurance" can be nuanced, and the rules can change. The Government of Canada page is the authoritative place to confirm how the eligibility line applies to your circumstances.

How they tend to work, side by side

Setting eligibility aside, the two sources of coverage behave somewhat differently. The comparison below is general — it describes typical structures, not any specific plan, and uses no invented figures. Your own private policy, or the current CDCP rules, will have the real numbers.

Feature Canadian Dental Care Plan (CDCP) Private dental insurance
Who it's for Canadian residents without access to private dental insurance, income-tested Anyone with an employer, group, personal or family policy
Eligibility basis No private coverage, adjusted family net income under $90,000, filed taxes, Canadian residency Enrolment through a job, association, pension or a purchased plan
What you pay Income-based co-payment; lower incomes may pay none, higher bands pay a share Varies by plan — commonly deductibles, a percentage of each service, and an annual maximum
What it helps with A defined range of eligible dental services; see official details Defined by the policy; often preventive, basic and some major services at differing rates
Where the rules live Government of Canada (Health Canada) Your plan booklet or benefits provider

What each tends to help with

In broad strokes, both the CDCP and most private plans are built around the same categories of dental care — the everyday preventive work like exams and cleanings, basic treatment such as fillings, and more involved procedures. What differs is the detail: which specific services are included, at what rate they're reimbursed, and what conditions or waiting periods apply. A private policy spells this out in its benefits booklet; the CDCP's covered services are described on the Government of Canada page. Neither should be assumed to cover everything, and in both cases a service being "covered" doesn't guarantee it's covered in full — which is why asking the dental office for specifics matters regardless of which applies to you.

Employer plans: the part that trips people up

A frequent question is whether a modest or barely used workplace dental benefit affects CDCP eligibility. In general, having access to an employer plan counts as having private coverage — the test isn't how good the plan is or whether you claim on it. If your employment situation changes and you lose that access, your eligibility picture may change too. Again, the specifics are set out officially, and it's worth re-checking after any change in employment or benefits.

Life changes are the usual trigger for a fresh look. Retiring, leaving a job, aging off a parent's plan, or a change in a spouse's benefits can all move you from one side of the eligibility line to the other. If any of those happen, it's worth revisiting the question rather than assuming your status from a year or two ago still holds.

What to ask, whichever applies to you

Whether you're covered privately or through the CDCP, a few questions keep costs predictable:

  • Is this service covered, and at what rate? Coverage of a service doesn't always mean it's covered in full.
  • What will I owe out of pocket? Ask the dental office for an estimate before treatment begins.
  • Are there annual limits? Private plans in particular often cap the total paid per year.
  • Does this office participate? For the CDCP, confirm the office takes part; for private insurance, confirm how direct billing works.

One more habit is worth building regardless of your coverage: keep your own records. Note what treatment you've had and when, hang on to estimates and statements, and track any annual limit you're working against on a private plan. People with coverage sometimes discover late in the year that they've hit a maximum, or that a service they assumed was covered wasn't — both of which are easier to plan around when you've been keeping a simple running tally rather than reconstructing it after the fact.

If you have specific coverage questions about your own situation, the clearest path is usually to speak to someone directly. A dental office can review an estimate with you, and for CDCP matters you can bring questions about coverage to a participating practice — while the official Government of Canada page remains the source of truth for eligibility and program rules.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have both the CDCP and private dental insurance?

Generally no. The CDCP is for Canadian residents without access to private dental insurance. Having access to a private plan usually makes you ineligible, even if you don't use it. Confirm current rules with the Government of Canada.

If my employer offers dental benefits, does that affect eligibility?

Yes. Access to an employer dental plan generally counts as private coverage. The key question is whether you have access to a plan, not whether you currently claim on it. Check the official criteria for your situation.

Do both have limits and co-pays?

In general terms, yes. The CDCP uses an income-based co-payment; private plans commonly have deductibles, percentage coverage and annual maximums. Amounts vary, so review your plan documents or the Government of Canada page.
