Dental Cleaning vs. Deep Cleaning: What's the Difference?

If a dentist has suggested a "deep cleaning," it's reasonable to wonder how it differs from the cleaning you're used to — and why it costs and takes more.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 8 min read

A routine cleaning and a deep cleaning are different procedures with different goals. A routine cleaning (prophylaxis) maintains gums that are already healthy; a deep cleaning — known clinically as scaling and root planing — treats gums where disease has begun to take hold below the gumline. One is maintenance; the other is treatment.

The names sound like two intensities of the same thing, which is where the confusion starts. In practice they address different conditions, use the gumline as a dividing marker, and often differ in how long they take, whether freezing is used, and how many visits are involved.

The routine cleaning: maintenance for healthy gums

A routine cleaning is the familiar six-month hygiene visit. The clinician removes plaque and tartar from the crowns of the teeth and the area at and just beneath the gumline, then polishes and often applies fluoride. It assumes the supporting structures underneath are essentially sound — the point is to keep them that way.

This works well for mouths where the gums are healthy or only mildly inflamed. The build-up hasn't migrated far below the gumline, so a standard scaling and polish keeps everything in check.

The deep cleaning: treatment for gums below the line

When gum disease progresses, the gum tissue begins to pull away from the teeth, creating deeper spaces called pockets. Plaque and tartar collect inside these pockets and onto the root surfaces of the teeth — places a routine cleaning simply cannot reach. Left alone, this fuels further inflammation and can lead to loss of the bone that holds teeth in place.

Scaling and root planing is the response. Scaling removes the hardened deposits from below the gumline and off the root; root planing smooths the root surface so the gum tissue can reattach more cleanly and pockets have less to cling to. The goal is to calm the inflammation and give the gums a chance to recover.

Why It Matters

Gum disease is largely silent in its earlier stages — often no pain, just some bleeding or puffiness. That's why the decision to recommend a deep cleaning rests on measurements and X-rays rather than on how your mouth feels.

Side by side

  Routine Cleaning (Prophylaxis) Deep Cleaning (Scaling & Root Planing)
Purpose Maintain healthy gums; prevent problems Treat active gum disease below the gumline
Where it works Tooth crowns, at and just below the gumline Deep gum pockets and root surfaces
Anaesthetic Not usually needed Local anaesthetic (freezing) often used
Visits Typically one short visit Often split across more than one visit, by area
Who it's for Healthy or mildly inflamed gums People with gum disease and deeper pockets
After Resume normal routine right away Possible short-term tenderness; follow-up checks

Why local anaesthetic and multiple visits?

Because a deep cleaning works beneath the gumline and against the root, the area is more sensitive than during a surface cleaning. Freezing a section of the mouth keeps the work comfortable. For the same reason, the treatment is frequently divided — for example, one half of the mouth at one appointment and the other half at the next — so you aren't numb everywhere at once and each area gets thorough attention.

Who actually needs a deep cleaning?

This is a clinical decision, not a self-diagnosis. A dentist or hygienist measures the depth of the gum pockets, reviews X-rays for bone changes, and looks for signs such as persistent bleeding, gum recession or shifting teeth. When those findings point to gum disease that has moved below the gumline, scaling and root planing becomes the appropriate step rather than another routine polish.

If you're unsure where you stand, that's exactly what an examination is for. A thorough assessment — including the pocket measurements described above — will show whether routine care is enough or whether scaling and root planing is warranted. It's genuinely worth asking rather than guessing, because treating gum disease earlier is almost always simpler than treating it later.

After a deep cleaning

Some tenderness or sensitivity for a day or two is common, and your dentist may suggest a gentle routine while the gums settle. Follow-up visits usually re-measure the pockets to see how the gums have responded. For many people, successful treatment shifts them onto a periodontal maintenance schedule — more frequent cleanings tailored to keeping the disease from returning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't I just get a regular cleaning instead of a deep cleaning?

A routine cleaning works above and just below the gumline on healthy or mildly affected gums. When deeper pockets and tartar have formed on the root surfaces, a routine cleaning can't reach them — which is the situation a deep cleaning is designed to treat. A dentist determines which you need based on an examination.

Is a deep cleaning done with freezing?

Often, yes. Because the work goes below the gumline and onto the root, local anaesthetic is commonly used to keep the area comfortable, frequently one section of the mouth at a time over more than one visit.

How do I know if I need scaling and root planing?

It's a clinical judgement based on gum-pocket measurements, X-rays and signs of gum disease — not something to self-diagnose. A dentist or hygienist assesses whether the condition of your gums calls for it.
