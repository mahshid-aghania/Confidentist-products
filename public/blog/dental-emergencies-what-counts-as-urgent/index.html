Dental Emergencies: What Counts as Urgent and What Can Wait

Not every dental problem is an emergency — but a few genuinely are. Knowing the difference helps you act quickly when it matters and stay calm when it doesn't.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

A dental emergency is any problem that needs prompt professional attention to stop pain, control bleeding, treat infection, or save a tooth. A few situations go further — they're medical emergencies that belong in a hospital. Most dental problems, though, are urgent-soon or routine and can wait for a timely appointment. The trick is telling them apart.

This guide sorts common situations into three tiers so you can decide where to go and how fast. It is general information, not a diagnosis. If you're unsure how serious something is, call a dentist or a health line and describe what's happening — when in doubt, get it checked.

Tier 1 — True emergencies: get care now

These can threaten your health, not just your tooth. Some belong in a hospital emergency department rather than a dental chair:

  • Facial swelling that is spreading, affecting your eye or neck, or making it hard to breathe or swallow.
  • Significant trauma to the face, jaw or head — especially with a possible fracture, loss of consciousness, or a deep cut.
  • Uncontrolled bleeding from the mouth that doesn't slow after firm pressure with gauze.
  • A knocked-out (avulsed) adult tooth — time-critical, with the best outcomes within the first hour.
  • Signs of a spreading infection: high fever, feeling very unwell, or swelling with difficulty opening the mouth.

Tier 2 — Urgent-soon: call your dentist promptly

These need attention within a day or two to prevent things getting worse, but they aren't usually dangerous:

  • A cracked or chipped tooth without severe pain or swelling.
  • A lost filling or crown, or a loosened one.
  • A persistent toothache that's uncomfortable but not severe, with no swelling or fever.
  • A broken denture or orthodontic wire that's irritating but not causing injury.
  • A small gum abscess or sore spot that isn't spreading.

Tier 3 — Routine: book a regular appointment

These are worth addressing but not pressing:

  • Mild, occasional sensitivity to hot or cold.
  • A chipped tooth edge with no pain.
  • Mild gum tenderness or minor bleeding when brushing.
  • A dull ache that comes and goes without other symptoms.

A quick triage table

Situation Urgency Where to go
Swelling affecting breathing or swallowing; spreading to eye/neck; high fever Emergency Hospital emergency department
Facial/jaw trauma, possible fracture, head injury Emergency Hospital emergency department
Knocked-out adult tooth Emergency (within ~1 hour) Dentist / urgent dental care
Uncontrolled bleeding after pressure Emergency Hospital emergency department
Severe, constant toothache with swelling or fever Urgent — same day Dentist (ER if swelling spreads)
Cracked tooth, lost filling or crown, moderate ache Urgent-soon — 1–2 days Dentist
Mild sensitivity, painless chip, minor gum tenderness Routine Dentist, next available visit
Hospital vs. Dentist

A rule of thumb: hospital emergency departments manage the medical dangers — airway, severe infection, bleeding, trauma — but generally don't do definitive dental repair. Expect them to stabilise you, manage pain and infection, and refer you to a dentist to treat the tooth itself.

Why a quick phone call usually comes first

For almost everything short of a life-threatening emergency, the best first move is a phone call rather than a drive. A dental office can often judge over the phone how serious a problem is, whether you need to be seen that day, and what to do in the meantime. Describing your symptoms clearly helps: when it started, whether there's swelling, how severe the pain is on a scale of one to ten, whether you have a fever, and whether anything makes it better or worse. That five-minute conversation frequently saves an unnecessary trip — or tells you to come in immediately.

It also helps to be specific about swelling, because swelling is the symptom that most often shifts a problem from one tier to another. A small, stable gum bump is different from swelling that is growing by the hour, changing the shape of your face, or closing your eye. The second pattern can mean an infection is spreading, and spreading infections are the main reason a dental problem becomes a hospital matter.

Who is seen, and how fast

Not every clinic handles urgent problems the same way. Some reserve same-day slots for existing patients in pain; some will see new patients in an emergency; some direct after-hours calls to a recorded message with instructions. None of this is obvious until you need it, which is exactly why it's worth asking your own dentist in advance how they handle emergencies and keeping that information somewhere you can find it quickly. If you're travelling or don't have a regular dentist, a local clinic offering urgent care or a telephone health line can point you in the right direction.

What you can safely do while you arrange care

Safe first aid buys time; it doesn't replace treatment. You can rinse gently with warm salt water, use a cold compress on the outside of the cheek to ease swelling, control bleeding by biting on clean gauze, and take over-the-counter pain relief as directed on the package. Save any pieces of a broken tooth. Do not attempt home dentistry — no drilling, no filing, and never place an aspirin tablet against the gum, which can burn the tissue rather than help. If symptoms escalate, move up a tier.

A few more small measures help in the hours before you're seen. Keep your head slightly elevated, even when resting, as lying flat can make throbbing worse. Stick to soft, lukewarm foods and chew on the opposite side. If you have a knocked-out adult tooth, keep it moist in milk and bring it with you rather than leaving it to dry out. And resist the temptation to repeatedly poke or test a sore tooth — handling it often only aggravates the area. None of these steps treat the underlying problem, but they keep you comfortable and avoid making things worse while professional care is arranged.

Knowing your options before you need them

The calmest time to find out how to reach urgent help is before an emergency. It's worth knowing your dentist's after-hours arrangements and keeping the number handy. A clinic that offers emergency dental care can advise over the phone whether you need to be seen that day, the next, or sent to hospital.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I go to a hospital emergency department instead of a dentist?

Go to a hospital for swelling that affects breathing or swallowing or spreads toward the eye or neck, a high fever with a mouth infection, uncontrolled bleeding, or facial and head trauma. Hospitals manage the medical danger; a dentist treats the tooth once you're stable.

Is a toothache always an emergency?

No. A mild, intermittent ache can usually wait for a prompt appointment. Severe, constant or throbbing pain — or pain with swelling, fever, or that keeps you awake — should be seen urgently, as it may point to infection.

What if a filling or crown falls out?

That's usually urgent-soon, not an emergency. Keep the crown if you can, avoid chewing on that side, keep the area clean, and call your dentist for a prompt visit. Don't try to glue it back with household adhesives.
