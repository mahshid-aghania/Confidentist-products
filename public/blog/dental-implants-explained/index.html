Dental Implants Explained: Parts, Process and What They Replace

Behind a natural-looking replacement tooth is a small piece of engineering with three parts and one quiet trick that sets implants apart from every other option.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 8 min read

A dental implant is not a single object you screw into place like a bolt. It is a small system of three parts — a post set into the jaw, a connector, and a replacement tooth on top — designed so that one of those parts does something no bridge or denture can: it stands in for the missing tooth root.

That distinction matters more than it first sounds. Most ways of replacing a tooth rebuild only the part you can see. An implant rebuilds the part you cannot — the anchor hidden in the bone — and everything else follows from there. Understanding how the pieces fit together makes the rest of the process far easier to picture.

The three parts, from the bone up

It helps to think of an implant the way you'd think of a tooth: there is a root, a neck, and a crown. An implant mirrors that layout with manufactured parts.

1. The fixture (the artificial root)

The fixture is a small post, usually made of medical-grade titanium or a titanium alloy, placed into the jawbone where the natural root used to be. It is the foundation of the whole restoration. Titanium is used because the body tolerates it well and because bone will bond to its surface — the single property that makes implants possible.

2. The abutment (the connector)

Once the fixture is stable, an abutment is attached to it. This is the piece that passes through the gum and gives the final tooth something to sit on. Depending on the case, it may be fitted at the time of placement or later, after healing.

3. The crown or restoration (the visible tooth)

The part you see and chew with is the crown, custom-made to match the shape and shade of neighbouring teeth. On larger cases the top restoration might instead be a bridge or a denture, but the principle is the same: it clips or screws onto the abutment.

Worth Knowing

Only the crown is visible in the mouth. The fixture and abutment stay below the gumline, which is why a well-made implant can look and feel much like a natural tooth even though two-thirds of it is hidden.

Osseointegration, in plain terms

The word sounds technical, but the idea is simple. After the fixture is placed, living bone gradually grows against its surface and locks onto it, like roots of a plant gripping soil. That bond is called osseointegration, and it is what allows an implant to withstand the forces of biting and chewing.

It is not instant. Osseointegration typically unfolds over several weeks to a few months, which is why implant treatment usually involves a healing period rather than a single appointment. During that time the bone and fixture become, in effect, one unit. How quickly and completely this happens varies from person to person and can only be judged through clinical examination and imaging.

Why replacing the root changes everything

When a tooth is lost, the jawbone that once supported its root no longer receives the day-to-day pressure of chewing. Over time that bone tends to shrink — a process called resorption. Replacing only the visible crown, as a conventional bridge or denture does, leaves the underlying bone without that stimulation.

Because an implant fixture sits in the bone and transmits chewing forces into it, it continues to give the jaw a reason to maintain itself in that area. This is the practical meaning of "replacing the root," and it is the core reason implants are discussed differently from other tooth-replacement options.

Single, multiple and implant-supported options

Implants are not only for one-at-a-time gaps. The same building blocks scale up.

  • Single tooth: one fixture, one abutment, one crown — used when a single tooth is missing and the neighbours are healthy.
  • Several teeth: two or more fixtures can support a fixed bridge that spans a run of missing teeth without touching the natural teeth on either side.
  • Full arch (implant-supported dentures): a small number of fixtures can anchor a denture so it snaps securely into place or is fixed permanently, rather than resting on the gums and relying on suction or adhesive.

Which of these suits a given mouth depends on how many teeth are missing, the amount and quality of available bone, overall health, and personal preference. None of it can be decided from a photo or a description — it comes out of an examination, X-rays and often a CBCT scan.

What an implant is not

An implant is not a quick fix, and it is not right for everyone. Candidacy depends on healthy gums, adequate bone (which can sometimes be built up first), and general health factors that a dentist or dental surgeon will review. It is also not maintenance-free: like natural teeth, implants rely on good daily cleaning and regular professional care to stay healthy.

What it is, when suitable, is a way to rebuild a tooth from the root up rather than bridging over the gap — and that is what makes it worth understanding part by part.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three parts of a dental implant?

The fixture (a titanium post placed in the jaw that acts as an artificial root), the abutment (a connector that passes through the gum), and the crown or other restoration — the visible tooth you chew with.

What does osseointegration mean?

It is the process by which living bone grows against and bonds to the implant fixture over several weeks to months, anchoring it firmly in the jaw so it can handle chewing forces.

Can one implant replace more than one tooth?

A single implant usually carries one crown, but a few well-placed fixtures can support a bridge across several teeth or secure a full-arch denture. The right setup depends on an individual clinical and radiographic assessment.
