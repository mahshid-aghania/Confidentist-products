Dental Implants vs. Bridges: Comparing Two Ways to Replace a Tooth

Both can fill a single gap convincingly. The real differences are in what they ask of the teeth around the gap, the bone beneath it, and the years ahead.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 8 min read

When one tooth is missing, two options dominate the conversation: a dental implant or a fixed bridge. Both restore a natural-looking tooth. The decision usually comes down to a handful of practical differences — chiefly whether you want to involve the neighbouring teeth, and how the choice affects the bone over time.

Neither is the "winner" in every mouth. A bridge can be the sensible choice in one situation and the wrong one in another. What follows is a fair look at how they differ, so a conversation with your dentist starts from an informed place.

The core difference: what each one leans on

A dental implant is a standalone replacement. A titanium post is placed in the jaw where the root used to be, and a crown is built on top. It does not touch the teeth on either side.

A traditional bridge works differently. The replacement tooth (the "pontic") is suspended between two crowns, and those crowns are cemented onto the natural teeth flanking the gap. To fit them, those teeth must be filed down — even if they are perfectly healthy. The bridge leans on its neighbours; the implant stands on its own.

Effect on neighbouring teeth

This is often the deciding factor. Preparing healthy teeth for a bridge is irreversible: once enamel is reduced, those teeth will always need a crown of some kind. If the adjacent teeth already have large fillings or crowns, that preparation matters less — the bridge may even be an efficient use of teeth that needed restoring anyway. If they are pristine, many patients prefer to leave them untouched, which favours an implant.

Effect on the jawbone

After a tooth is lost, the bone that held its root tends to shrink because it no longer receives chewing pressure. An implant transmits that pressure into the bone and helps give the jaw a reason to maintain itself in the area. A bridge spans the gap above the gum and does not stimulate the bone underneath, so some gradual bone loss beneath a bridge is common over the years.

Worth Knowing

Because an implant needs enough healthy bone to anchor into, and a bridge does not, bone volume often steers the decision. Where bone has already been lost, grafting may be suggested before an implant — or a bridge may be the more straightforward route.

Side-by-side comparison

Consideration Dental implant Fixed bridge
Adjacent teeth Left untouched Filed down to take anchoring crowns
Jawbone Stimulated; helps preserve bone Not stimulated beneath the pontic
Treatment time Longer — includes a healing period for osseointegration Shorter — often completed in a few weeks
Surgery Minor surgical placement required No surgery; tooth preparation only
Upfront cost Typically higher per tooth Often lower upfront
Cleaning Brushed and flossed like a natural tooth Needs special flossing under the pontic
Longevity Can last many years with good care Can last many years; relies on health of anchor teeth

Cost, candidacy and time

A bridge is often less expensive to place at the outset and is usually completed faster, since there is no surgical healing period. An implant generally costs more per tooth upfront and takes longer overall because the bone needs time to bond to the fixture. Over a long horizon the comparison can shift, since a bridge depends on the continued health of the teeth supporting it, but individual circumstances vary too much to promise one is "cheaper in the end."

Candidacy also differs. An implant needs enough healthy bone and gum, reasonable general health, and often a CBCT scan to plan. A bridge needs sound teeth on either side of the gap. A dentist or dental surgeon weighs these factors together — no single rule applies to everyone.

Maintenance over the years

An implant crown is cleaned much like a natural tooth, with regular brushing and flossing, plus professional maintenance to keep the surrounding gum healthy. A bridge requires threading floss or a special tool beneath the pontic to clear the space the pontic can't reach — a habit worth mastering, because decay in the anchor teeth is a common reason bridges eventually fail.

So which should you consider?

If the teeth beside the gap are healthy and you want to protect both them and the bone, an implant is usually the option that keeps your options open longest. If those teeth already need crowns, or if surgery or a healing period isn't practical for you, a bridge can be a sound, efficient choice. The honest answer is that this is a decision to make with a clinician who has seen your X-rays — not from a table alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a bridge damage healthy teeth?

A traditional bridge requires the teeth on either side of the gap to be filed down for anchoring crowns. When those teeth are healthy, that irreversible preparation is a real trade-off. If they already have large fillings or crowns, the trade-off is smaller.

Is an implant always better than a bridge?

No. An implant spares neighbouring teeth and helps preserve bone, but it needs adequate bone and a healing period. A bridge can be faster and may suit cases where an implant isn't advisable. The right choice depends on an individual clinical and radiographic assessment.

Which lasts longer?

Both can last many years with good care. Longevity depends on hygiene, bite, general health and habits such as smoking, so no fixed lifespan can be promised. Your dentist can give a realistic expectation for your own mouth.
