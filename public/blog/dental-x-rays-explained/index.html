Dental X-Rays Explained: Types, Safety, and How Often You Need Them

X-rays let a dentist see what the eye can't — between teeth, under fillings and below the gumline. Here's what the different images show and how to think about safety.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 8 min read

Dental X-rays reveal the parts of your mouth a visual exam can't reach — decay hiding between teeth, bone levels around the roots, infections at the tip of a root, and the position of teeth that haven't erupted. There are several types, each suited to a different question, and modern low-dose digital imaging keeps the radiation involved small. How often you need them depends on your individual risk, not a fixed calendar.

A lot of unease about dental X-rays comes from not knowing what's being taken or why. Once you can tell a bitewing from a panoramic and understand how dentists think about dose and frequency, the whole topic becomes far less mysterious.

Why X-rays are part of an exam

A dentist can see the chewing surfaces and outer faces of your teeth directly, but much of what matters is out of view. The contact points between teeth, the interior beneath existing fillings, the bone that anchors the roots, and anything happening below the gumline are all invisible to the naked eye. X-rays fill that gap, often catching problems early — while they're small and simpler to manage.

The main types of dental X-ray

Different images answer different questions. Here are the four you're most likely to encounter.

Type What it shows Commonly used for
Bitewing The crowns of upper and lower back teeth together, and the bone level between them Detecting decay between teeth and checking bone around back teeth
Periapical A single tooth from crown to root tip, plus surrounding bone Investigating a specific tooth — pain, infection at the root, or an abscess
Panoramic A single wide, flat image of the whole mouth, jaws and sinuses An overview — wisdom teeth, jaw assessment, broad planning
CBCT (cone-beam CT) A 3-D view of teeth, bone and surrounding structures Detailed planning for implants, surgery or complex cases

Bitewings and periapicals: the everyday workhorses

Bitewing X-rays, named for the little tab you bite on, are the most routine. They show where decay most often starts — the tight contact points between teeth — and the bone level between back teeth. Periapical images zoom in on one tooth along its full length, which is what a dentist wants when a specific tooth is causing trouble.

Panoramic and CBCT: the big-picture views

A panoramic X-ray sweeps around the head to produce one broad image of both jaws — useful for seeing wisdom teeth, assessing the jaw, or getting an overview. CBCT produces a three-dimensional reconstruction and is reserved for situations where that extra detail genuinely changes the plan, such as implant placement or complex surgery. It involves more radiation than a bitewing, so it's used selectively, only when its information is needed.

Radiation and safety, in plain terms

We're all exposed to small amounts of natural background radiation every day — from the ground, the air and the sun. The dose from routine dental X-rays, especially bitewings, is small relative to that everyday background. Digital sensors, now standard in many practices, have reduced exposure further compared with the older film era.

Dentists work to a well-established principle: keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable. In practice that means taking an X-ray only when there's a clear clinical reason, choosing the smallest image that answers the question, and using protective measures such as shielding where appropriate. If you're pregnant or think you might be, tell your dental team so they can factor that in.

A Fair Question to Ask

It's completely reasonable to ask, "Why do I need this X-ray, and what are you hoping to see?" A good dentist welcomes the question and can explain how a given image relates to your care.

How often do you actually need them?

There is no universal interval. The right frequency is risk-based, decided by your dentist from factors such as your history of decay and gum disease, your age, whether you have symptoms, and how stable your mouth has been. Someone who gets frequent cavities or has active gum disease may need images more often; someone with a long run of healthy, stable teeth may go considerably longer between them. The guiding idea is need, not routine.

This individualised approach is exactly why X-rays are discussed within the context of a full examination rather than taken automatically every visit. If you'd like to understand how imaging fits into a check-up, you can read more about how dental exams and X-rays work together as part of preventive care.

The takeaway

Dental X-rays are a targeted tool, not a blanket procedure. Each type answers a specific question, modern imaging keeps doses low, and frequency should reflect your personal risk. Understanding that framework makes it easier to have an open conversation with your dentist — which is where any decision about your own imaging should happen. This article is general education and not a substitute for that individual advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are dental X-rays safe?

Modern dental X-rays use low doses of radiation, and digital systems have reduced exposure further compared with older film. Dentists follow safety principles — shielding where appropriate and taking images only when there's a clear reason — to keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable.

How often should I have dental X-rays?

There's no single interval for everyone. Frequency is based on your individual risk — things like your decay and gum-disease history, age and current concerns. Someone at higher risk may need images more often than someone with stable, healthy teeth. Your dentist decides based on need, not a fixed schedule.

Can I refuse X-rays or ask to space them out?

You can always discuss the reasoning with your dentist. X-rays are recommended when they're expected to provide information that affects your care. A good dentist will explain why a particular image is suggested and how it fits your risk, so you can make an informed decision together.
