Dentures and Aging: Fit, Comfort, and Alternatives

Dentures that fit well years ago can feel different over time — and that change is both normal and manageable. Here's why it happens and what your options are.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

If your dentures have started to slip, rock, or rub sore spots after years of sitting comfortably, nothing has gone wrong with them — your mouth has simply changed. Understanding that one fact takes a lot of worry out of the situation and points toward the right fix.

Why dentures loosen over time

A conventional denture rests on the gum ridge and the bone underneath it. When natural teeth are removed, the jawbone that once held them is no longer stimulated and slowly resorbs — it shrinks and flattens over the years. Your denture was shaped to fit the ridge as it was on the day it was made. As the bone beneath changes, the fit drifts, and the appliance that once suctioned snugly begins to move.

This is gradual and expected. It's also the main reason dentures aren't a one-time, lifelong purchase but something that needs periodic attention.

Bone resorption is usually fastest in the first year or so after teeth are removed and then slows, but it never entirely stops. That's why a denture made right after extractions almost always needs refitting within the first year — the mouth it was built for is changing underneath it. It's not a sign anything was done poorly; it's simply the biology of a healing, remodelling ridge.

Relines and remakes

Two common solutions address a loosening fit, and they're not the same thing.

A reline refits the inner (tissue) surface of your existing denture to the current shape of your gums. The teeth and overall denture stay the same; only the part that contacts your gums is rebuilt so it hugs the ridge again. Relines are the usual first response to looseness and can be repeated over the life of a denture.

A remake is a new denture. It's considered when the appliance itself is worn — teeth ground flat, acrylic cracked or stained — or when the ridge has changed so much that a reline can't restore a good fit. Dentures don't last forever; replacement after several years is routine, not a failure.

Worth Knowing

Resist the urge to fix a loose denture permanently with over-the-counter glue or DIY reline kits. They can mask a fit problem, irritate the gums, and make a proper professional adjustment harder. Adhesive is fine as a short-term aid; it isn't a substitute for a reline.

Sore spots and daily care

A new or newly relined denture can create pressure points as your tissues adjust — small, tender sore spots where the acrylic presses too hard. These are common and usually resolved with a quick adjustment, so don't tough them out for weeks. Keeping up good habits helps too: clean dentures daily with a denture brush and cleanser (not abrasive toothpaste), take them out overnight to let the tissues rest, and keep seeing your dentist even when you have no natural teeth left — the gums, ridge and soft tissues still need checking.

Comparing the main options

For someone replacing missing teeth or an ageing denture, a few paths exist. The right one depends entirely on an individual clinical examination — the table below is a general orientation, not a recommendation.

Option How it stays in General considerations
Conventional full denture Rests on the gum ridge; suction and (optionally) adhesive Non-surgical and widely used; fit changes as bone resorbs, so relines or remakes are expected over time.
Partial denture Clasps onto remaining natural teeth Used when some natural teeth remain; relies on those teeth staying healthy.
Implant-retained denture Snaps onto a few dental implants placed in the jaw More stable and can slow bone loss at the implant sites; requires minor surgery and adequate bone and health, confirmed by examination.
Fixed implant bridge Permanently attached to several implants Feels closest to natural teeth and isn't removed daily; a larger treatment and investment, with suitability determined clinically.

Why fit matters beyond comfort

A loose or poorly fitting denture isn't only uncomfortable — over time it can cause other problems. Constant rubbing can irritate the gums and create persistent sore spots or inflammation. Chewing with an unstable denture is harder and less efficient, which can nudge people toward softer, less nutritious food. And a denture that shifts while speaking or laughing chips away at confidence in social settings. These are good reasons to treat a loosening fit as something to address promptly rather than endure.

Adjusting to eating and speaking

Whether a denture is new or newly refitted, a short adjustment period is normal. Start with soft foods cut into smaller pieces and chew on both sides to keep the denture balanced. Speech can feel odd at first — reading aloud for a few minutes a day helps your tongue and cheeks relearn the shape. Most people find that what feels strange in week one feels unremarkable within a few weeks.

A few practical habits smooth the transition. Introduce tougher or chewier foods gradually rather than all at once. Expect more saliva than usual in the first days — that settles. And if a particular spot stays sore past the initial adjustment, don't simply live with it; a quick visit to ease the pressure point is routine and prevents a small irritation from becoming an ulcer.

Caring for the denture between adjustments

Day-to-day care does a lot to keep a denture comfortable and lasting. Rinse it after meals, brush it daily with a soft denture brush and a non-abrasive cleanser, and handle it over a folded towel or a basin of water so a drop doesn't crack it. Leaving dentures out overnight in water gives the gums a rest and helps prevent irritation. And don't let a denture dry out completely — acrylic can distort if it sits dry for long stretches. Simple habits like these won't stop bone resorption, but they keep the appliance in good shape between the professional relines it will eventually need.

When to ask about alternatives

If looseness, repeated sore spots, or difficulty eating are wearing on you despite relines, it's reasonable to ask whether an implant-retained option might help. For some older adults, anchoring a denture to a few implants restores a lot of confidence at mealtimes and in conversation. Whether you're a candidate is a clinical question — bone volume, general health and personal preference all factor in.

If you'd like to understand what's realistic for your situation, a practice that offers the full range of denture options — from relines and remakes to implant-supported solutions — can examine your mouth and walk you through what each path would actually involve.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my dentures feel looser than they used to?

After teeth are lost, the jawbone gradually resorbs. A denture made for the old ridge shape slowly loosens as the bone shrinks beneath it. This is normal and is usually handled with a reline or remake.

What's the difference between a reline and a remake?

A reline refits the inner surface of your existing denture to your current gums. A remake is a brand-new denture, considered when the appliance is worn or the ridge has changed too much for a reline to help.

Are implant-retained dentures an option for older adults?

Often, yes. A few implants can anchor a denture so it snaps securely in place rather than resting on the gums. Suitability depends on bone, health and preference, which a clinical examination determines.
