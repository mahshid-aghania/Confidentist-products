Gingivitis vs. Periodontitis: Understanding the Difference

The two terms get used almost interchangeably, but they describe very different situations — and the difference between them often comes down to whether the damage can still be undone.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

Gingivitis and periodontitis sit on the same spectrum, but they are not the same thing. Gingivitis is inflammation of the gums, and it is usually reversible. Periodontitis is what can happen when that inflammation is left to progress — it reaches the bone and tissue that anchor your teeth, and that loss is generally permanent. Understanding where the line falls explains why early action matters so much.

Both conditions begin the same way: with plaque, the sticky layer of bacteria that forms on teeth throughout the day. When plaque is not removed, it irritates the gums and the body responds with inflammation. What happens next — whether it stays a gum problem or becomes a bone problem — is the crux of the distinction.

Gingivitis: inflammation you can usually turn around

Gingivitis is the milder, earlier condition. It affects only the gum tissue — the soft tissue around the base of the teeth — and does not involve the underlying bone. Common signs include gums that are red or puffy, that bleed when you brush or floss, and that may feel tender. Importantly, the teeth themselves remain firmly supported.

The reason gingivitis is described as reversible is that the structures holding your teeth in place have not been damaged. With thorough daily cleaning and a professional cleaning to remove hardened deposits, the inflammation often settles and the gums return to health. It is one of the few dental problems where diligent home care genuinely makes a large difference.

Periodontitis: when the damage reaches the foundation

If gingivitis is not addressed, inflammation can extend deeper. In periodontitis, the gum begins to pull away from the tooth, forming spaces called pockets. These pockets trap bacteria below the gum line where a toothbrush cannot reach. Over time, the inflammatory process can break down the bone and connective tissue that hold teeth in place.

This is the key difference: the bone and attachment that are lost in periodontitis do not grow back on their own. The condition can be managed and stabilised, but it cannot simply be reversed the way gingivitis can. Signs of more advanced disease may include receding gums, persistent bad breath, teeth that feel loose, or changes in how the teeth fit together.

The Core Distinction

Gingivitis affects the gums and is usually reversible. Periodontitis affects the bone and attachment that support the teeth, and that loss is generally not reversible — only manageable. The dividing line is whether the supporting structures have been affected.

How one becomes the other

Gingivitis does not inevitably become periodontitis — many cases resolve and never progress. But when inflammation persists unchecked, the risk of progression rises. The transition is gradual rather than sudden: pockets deepen slightly, a little bone is lost, and the process continues quietly because, like early gum disease generally, it is often painless.

Several factors can influence whether and how quickly gingivitis advances, including the thoroughness of home care, smoking, certain health conditions, and individual susceptibility. Because progression is possible but not guaranteed, treating gingivitis promptly is a way of keeping the problem on the reversible side of the line.

A side-by-side comparison

  Gingivitis Periodontitis
What is affected The gum tissue only The gums plus the supporting bone and attachment
Reversible? Usually, with good care Generally not — damage can be managed, not undone
Typical signs Red, puffy gums; bleeding when brushing or flossing Receding gums, deep pockets, loose teeth, persistent bad breath
Pockets around teeth Shallow; within a normal range Deeper pockets form as attachment is lost
Typical approach Improved home care plus professional cleaning Deeper cleaning below the gum line and ongoing maintenance; surgery in some cases

The table is a general guide, not a diagnostic tool. Only a clinical examination — which includes measuring the spaces around each tooth and often taking X-rays — can establish which condition is present and how far it has progressed.

How treatment differs

Because the two conditions are different, so is their management. Gingivitis is typically handled with a professional cleaning to remove plaque and hardened tartar, paired with coaching on home technique. Once the irritant is gone and good habits are in place, the gums often recover.

Periodontitis usually calls for more. A common first step is a deeper cleaning that reaches below the gum line to clear bacteria from the pockets and smooth the root surfaces so the gums can reattach as much as possible. Some situations benefit from surgical treatment to reduce pockets or address bone loss. And because the condition tends to be ongoing, regular maintenance visits become an important part of keeping it stable. Decisions about gum disease treatment always depend on an individual assessment by a qualified professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is gingivitis the same as periodontitis?

No. Gingivitis is inflammation limited to the gums and is usually reversible with good home care and professional cleaning. Periodontitis is more advanced, affecting the bone and tissue that support the teeth, and that damage is generally not reversible. Gingivitis can progress to periodontitis if left unaddressed.

Does gingivitis always turn into periodontitis?

Not necessarily. Many cases of gingivitis resolve with improved oral hygiene and a professional cleaning and never advance. Whether it progresses depends on factors such as home care, individual risk, smoking and overall health. Because progression is possible but not inevitable, addressing gingivitis early is the sensible approach.

Can periodontitis be cured?

Periodontitis cannot be reversed in the sense of regrowing lost bone and attachment on its own, but it can usually be managed. Treatment aims to control the infection, halt further loss and keep things stable over time. Ongoing maintenance matters, and the right plan depends on an individual clinical examination.
