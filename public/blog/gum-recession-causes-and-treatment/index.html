Gum Recession: Causes, Consequences, and Treatment Options

When gums pull back, teeth can start to look longer and feel more sensitive. The reasons are varied — and because the cause shapes the fix, understanding them is the first step.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

Gum recession is the gradual loss of gum tissue that normally hugs the base of each tooth. As the gum line retreats, more of the tooth — and eventually the root — becomes exposed. It tends to happen slowly and quietly, which is why many people only notice once a tooth looks longer than it used to or a cold drink produces an unexpected zing. The encouraging news is that recession can often be slowed or stopped once its cause is understood.

There is rarely a single villain. Recession is usually the product of one or more factors acting over time, and the most effective response depends on which ones are at play.

What causes gums to recede

Brushing too hard

It is a common surprise that overly vigorous brushing can wear away gum tissue rather than protect it. A hard-bristled brush, a heavy hand, or a scrubbing motion can abrade the delicate gum margin over months and years. Recession from this cause often shows up on the outer surfaces of the teeth, sometimes more on one side depending on which hand holds the brush.

Gum (periodontal) disease

When inflammation progresses and affects the bone and attachment that support the teeth, the gum line can recede as a consequence. In this situation recession is a sign of a deeper process rather than a standalone problem, which is why it should prompt a full assessment rather than being treated in isolation.

Grinding and clenching

Habitual grinding or clenching — often during sleep — places repeated stress on the teeth and their supporting tissues. For some people this contributes to recession, and it may also wear the teeth and cause sensitivity. Addressing the habit, sometimes with a night guard, can be part of the picture.

Naturally thin gum tissue

Some people simply have thinner, more delicate gum tissue, which is more prone to receding. This is partly a matter of individual anatomy and genetics. Thin tissue does not cause recession by itself, but it can make the gums more vulnerable to the other factors on this list.

Tooth position and orthodontics

A tooth that sits slightly outside the arch has less bone and gum covering it to begin with, which can predispose it to recession. Orthodontic treatment that moves teeth can also change the balance of tissue around them. This is one reason orthodontic planning takes the health and thickness of the gums into account.

Worth Knowing

Because recession can stem from very different causes — a brushing habit, gum disease, grinding, anatomy — two people with similar-looking recession may need quite different care. Identifying the underlying driver is the part of an assessment that shapes everything that follows.

Why recession matters

Beyond appearance, recession has practical consequences. The exposed root surface is not covered by enamel and is more sensitive, so cold air, cold or sweet foods, and brushing can trigger discomfort. Exposed roots are also somewhat more vulnerable to decay and to wear. And because recession can be a signal of gum disease, ignoring it may mean missing a problem that is progressing beneath the surface. None of this is cause for alarm, but it is reason enough to have receding gums looked at rather than watched indefinitely.

Treatment options

Treatment ranges from simple adjustments to surgical procedures, and the right choice depends on the cause, the extent of recession and your symptoms.

Managing sensitivity

When the main complaint is sensitivity from exposed roots, desensitising toothpastes and in-office treatments can often reduce the discomfort. These measures address the symptom rather than the recession itself, so they are usually paired with tackling whatever is driving the tissue loss.

Changing contributing habits

If aggressive brushing is the culprit, switching to a soft-bristled brush and a gentler technique can stop further recession — sometimes that alone is enough. Where grinding plays a role, a night guard may help. Where gum disease is involved, treating the disease is the priority.

Gum grafting and surgical options

When recession is more pronounced, exposed roots need coverage, or the band of firm gum tissue has become very thin, a periodontist may recommend a gum graft. In broad terms, this procedure adds tissue to cover an exposed root or thicken the gum, with the aims of reducing sensitivity, protecting the root and restoring a more stable gum line. There are different grafting techniques, and whether grafting is suitable — and which approach fits — is decided through an individual clinical examination.

Receding gums are worth a professional opinion rather than self-management, precisely because the best response depends on the cause. A conversation about gum recession treatment with a qualified professional is the way to find out which options apply to you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do receding gums grow back?

Gum tissue that has receded does not regrow on its own. The goal of treatment is usually to stop further recession and, where appropriate, to cover exposed root surfaces with a procedure such as a gum graft. Whether coverage is possible depends on the cause and extent of the recession and is determined by a clinical examination.

Is gum recession always caused by gum disease?

No. Gum disease is one cause, but recession can also result from brushing too hard, grinding or clenching, naturally thin gum tissue, the position of a tooth, orthodontic movement and other factors. Because the cause shapes the right treatment, identifying it is an important part of any assessment.

Can gum recession be stopped without surgery?

In many cases, yes. If recession is mild and driven by a habit such as aggressive brushing, changing technique and addressing contributing factors may halt its progress. Sensitivity from exposed roots can often be managed with desensitising products. Surgery is considered when coverage or more substantial correction is needed, and only a professional can advise which path suits your situation.
