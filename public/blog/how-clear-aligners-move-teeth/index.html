How Clear Aligners Move Teeth: The Science in Plain Language

Those nearly invisible trays look passive, but they are quietly running a biological process that reshapes the bone around each tooth. Here is what is actually happening in your mouth.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

Clear aligners move teeth the same way braces do: by applying gentle, sustained pressure that tells the bone around a tooth to let go on one side and rebuild on the other. The difference is how that pressure is delivered — through a sequence of custom plastic trays rather than wires and brackets. The biology underneath is identical, and it is older than any orthodontic product.

It helps to picture a tooth not as something cemented into the jaw, but as something suspended in it. Each root sits in a socket, cushioned by a thin layer of ligament fibres. That ligament is the key player in everything that follows.

The quiet machinery of bone remodelling

When an aligner presses a tooth in a particular direction, the ligament on the leading side is compressed and the side behind it is stretched. The body reads this as a signal to renovate. On the compressed side, specialized cells called osteoclasts begin dissolving a sliver of bone, opening space for the tooth to drift into. On the stretched side, osteoblasts lay down new bone to fill the gap left behind.

This paired process — removal on one side, construction on the other — is called bone remodelling, and it is the entire reason teeth can be moved at all. It is slow by design. Push too hard and the blood supply to the ligament can be choked off, which actually stalls movement rather than speeding it up. Gentle and continuous beats forceful and intermittent every time.

Why gentle beats forceful

It is tempting to assume that pushing harder would move teeth faster, but the biology does not reward brute force. The ligament around each root carries a delicate blood supply, and the remodelling cells depend on it. Too much pressure compresses those vessels, starving the very cells that are supposed to be renovating bone. Movement slows, and in extreme cases the root or surrounding bone can be harmed.

This is why a well-designed aligner feels like steady, patient pressure rather than a vice. The goal is to stay in the sweet spot where cells are active and comfortable — firm enough to signal change, light enough to keep the tissue healthy. Light and continuous is the principle that governs the whole system.

Why movement is broken into stages

No single tray tries to do too much. A typical aligner is designed to move the target teeth only a fraction of a millimetre — often around a quarter of a millimetre — before it has done its job. Once the teeth catch up to the shape of that tray, it stops exerting meaningful force and it is time for the next one in the series.

Stringing dozens of these small steps together is how a crowded or gapped smile is gradually walked toward its planned position. Each tray is essentially a slightly corrected mould of where your teeth should be a week or two from now, and your teeth chase that shape until they arrive.

There is also a reason the trays feel snug when new and looser by the end of their stint. At first the tray is a slightly different shape than your current teeth, so it grips and pushes. As the teeth migrate into its shape, that mismatch disappears and the pressure fades — which is your cue that the step is complete and the next tray is ready to begin the cycle again.

Worth Knowing

Aligners are usually swapped on a schedule set by your dentist — commonly every one to two weeks — rather than when a tray "feels loose." That snug-then-loose feeling is the movement happening on schedule, and changing early or late can throw off the sequence.

What the attachments are for

Smooth plastic is excellent at tipping a tooth but poor at more complex moves — rotating a round tooth, pulling one down, or bodily shifting a root. That is where attachments come in: small tooth-coloured composite bumps bonded to specific teeth. They give the aligner a surface to push and pull against so it can deliver force in a precise direction instead of simply sliding off.

Because they are matched to the shade of your enamel, attachments are usually hard to spot from a conversational distance, though you will feel them with your tongue and see them up close. They are removed and polished off at the end of treatment.

How the plan is built before the first tray

Modern aligner treatment starts with a digital model of your mouth. Many clinics capture this with an intraoral scanner — a wand that builds a 3D picture of your teeth without the old putty impressions. From that scan, treatment-planning software maps out the staged journey from where your teeth are now to the target position, and the trays are manufactured from that sequence.

That software is a planning aid, not a crystal ball. The animated preview of a finished smile shows the goal the trays are engineered toward; it is not a promise. Biology, tooth and root shape, gum health and how faithfully the trays are worn all influence the real result, which is why progress is monitored and plans are sometimes refined partway through.

Why wear time is non-negotiable

Here is the part that surprises people: the technology does almost nothing if the trays are in a pocket. Because tooth movement depends on sustained force, the usual guidance is to wear aligners around 22 hours a day — effectively all the time except for eating and cleaning your teeth.

Skip hours here and there and two things happen. The current tray never fully expresses its planned movement, and the teeth can drift back slightly between wears, so the next tray no longer fits as intended. Compliance is not a nag; it is the mechanism. A thoughtfully designed plan and a disciplined wearer are a team, and the plastic cannot carry the project alone.

The short version

Aligners work by harnessing a natural remodelling process — applying light, steady pressure that coaxes bone to reshape around each root, one small staged step at a time, with attachments handling the trickier moves and digital planning charting the route. Whether this approach suits a particular bite is a clinical question that depends on an individual examination, not something a video or an app can decide. If you are curious whether your case is a fit, a consultation about clear aligner treatment is the place to get a real answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do clear aligners hurt?

Most people feel pressure or mild tenderness for a day or two after switching to a new tray, easing as the teeth respond. It is usually a sign the aligner is doing its job. Persistent or sharp pain is worth mentioning to your dentist.

Why do aligners have to be worn around 22 hours a day?

Tooth movement relies on steady, continuous force. Each tray is designed around near-constant wear, so long stretches without them interrupt the biology and can slow or stall progress — and may stop the next tray from fitting properly.

What are the little bumps bonded to my teeth?

Those are attachments — small tooth-coloured composite shapes that give the aligner something to grip, so it can apply force in a specific direction, such as rotating or uprighting a tooth. They are polished off when treatment ends.
