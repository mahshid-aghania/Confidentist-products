How Often Should Each Member of Your Family See the Dentist?

"Every six months" is a useful habit, not a scientific law. The right interval depends on who you are, how old you are, and what your mouth is actually doing.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 8 min read

There's no single number that fits a whole family. A healthy, low-risk adult and a six-year-old who is just learning to brush do not belong on the same schedule, and neither of them necessarily belongs on the rigid twice-a-year plan most of us grew up with. Modern dental practice leans toward risk-based recall — matching how often you're seen to how much supervision your mouth actually needs.

That doesn't mean "every six months" is wrong. For many people it's a perfectly reasonable rhythm. It just isn't a rule to be applied identically to a toddler, a teenager, a pregnant parent and a grandparent. Here's how the thinking shifts across a lifetime.

Why the calendar isn't the whole answer

A dentist decides how often to see you by weighing your personal risk: your history of cavities, the state of your gums, how well plaque is controlled at home, whether you smoke, your diet, dry mouth from medications, and conditions like diabetes. Two people the same age can sensibly land on very different intervals. Someone with spotless gums and no decay in years may be seen less often; someone with active gum disease may need a cleaning every few months.

So treat the guide below as a starting point. Your own dentist's recommendation, based on an examination, always takes precedence.

A rough map by life stage

Life stage Typical interval Why
Toddlers (first tooth to ~3) First visit by age 1, then as advised Early visits build comfort and catch problems before they grow; habits start forming now.
Children (~4–12) Usually every 6 months New teeth erupt quickly and decay can spread fast; regular checks keep pace with change.
Teenagers Every 6 months, sometimes more Braces, wisdom teeth, diet shifts and variable brushing raise the value of closer monitoring.
Adults (low risk) 6–12 months Stable, healthy mouths may safely stretch intervals on a dentist's advice.
Adults (higher risk) Every 3–4 months Gum disease, frequent decay, smoking or diabetes call for tighter supervision.
During pregnancy Maintain routine care Hormonal changes can inflame gums, so continuing regular care matters.
Seniors Every 6 months, often closer Dry mouth, root decay, dentures and medical conditions warrant steady attention.

The youngest patients

For babies and toddlers, the first appointment is less about treatment and more about familiarity — getting a child used to the chair and giving parents guidance on brushing and feeding. From there, most children settle into six-month checks, which keep pace with how quickly young teeth erupt and how fast decay can move in them.

Teenagers and the busy middle years

Teens often slip on oral hygiene just as braces, wisdom teeth and sugary diets raise their risk. Many benefit from regular six-month visits, sometimes more frequent ones during orthodontic treatment. Adults in their working years are where risk-based recall really earns its keep: a low-risk adult might reasonably move to annual check-ups, while someone fighting gum disease may need a hygienist every few months.

Worth Knowing

Pregnancy is a stage people often overlook. Hormonal changes can make gums more prone to inflammation and bleeding, so continuing routine dental care while pregnant is encouraged. Always tell your dental team you're expecting so they can plan accordingly.

Later life

Older adults carry a distinct set of risks: dry mouth from medications, decay around the roots of teeth, denture fit, and medical conditions that interact with oral health. Many seniors do best on steady six-month visits, and some closer than that. The goal is to catch small problems early, when they're easiest to manage.

The practical takeaway for a household

Rather than fixing the whole family on one date, it's worth asking your dentist what interval each person actually needs — then stacking appointments where you can. Frequently Asked Questions

Is twice a year always the right number of visits?

Not always. Two visits a year is a sensible default, but a dentist may suggest more or fewer based on your personal risk of decay and gum disease. Some low-risk adults are seen annually; higher-risk patients are seen more often.

When should a child first see the dentist?

The Canadian Dental Association suggests a first visit within six months of the first tooth appearing, or by one year of age — whichever comes first.

Do I need dental care differently during pregnancy?

Pregnancy can increase the risk of gum inflammation, so continuing routine dental care is encouraged. Let your dental team know you're pregnant so they can plan treatment appropriately.
