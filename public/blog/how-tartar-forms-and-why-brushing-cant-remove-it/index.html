How Tartar Forms — and Why Brushing Alone Can't Remove It

You brush twice a day and still get that rough, chalky build-up. It isn't a sign you're doing something wrong — it's chemistry, and it explains why cleanings exist.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 6 min read

Tartar begins as plaque — the soft bacterial film that forms on your teeth all day. If that plaque isn't cleared within a day or two, minerals from your saliva settle into it and harden it into a cement-like deposit called calculus. Once that happens, the deposit bonds to the tooth, and no amount of brushing will lift it. That's not a failure of effort; it's the reason professional cleanings are necessary.

It's one of the more counterintuitive facts about oral health: you can brush diligently and still accumulate tartar. Understanding the short journey from soft film to hard deposit makes the whole system of daily care plus regular cleanings make sense.

It starts with plaque — every single day

Your mouth is never sterile, and it isn't meant to be. Within hours of cleaning your teeth, a thin film of bacteria, saliva proteins and food particles begins to re-form on the enamel. This is plaque. It's soft, sticky and, crucially, removable — a toothbrush and floss clear it readily while it's fresh.

The catch is that plaque rebuilds constantly. Every meal and every hour feeds it. This is why "I brushed this morning" doesn't buy you the whole day, and why the spots you miss are the spots where trouble starts.

The hardening: how plaque becomes tartar

Here's the pivotal part. Saliva is rich in minerals, particularly calcium and phosphate — the same sorts of minerals that make up tooth structure. When plaque sits undisturbed, those minerals begin to deposit into it and crystallise. The soft film gradually hardens into tartar, also known as dental calculus.

This can begin within a day or two. Over time, layer builds on layer: fresh plaque collects on the rough tartar surface, mineralises in turn, and the deposit grows. What started as something you could wipe off becomes a hard, firmly attached crust.

The Key Point

The window matters more than the elbow grease. Daily brushing and flossing work because they remove plaque before it mineralises. Once that window closes and tartar forms, home tools can no longer win against it.

Why a toothbrush can't lift hardened tartar

A toothbrush is designed to sweep away soft, loose material. Tartar is neither — it's a hardened mineral deposit chemically bonded to the tooth surface, closer in nature to a thin layer of stone than to a sticky film. Bristles simply glide over it. Even stiffer brushes or harder scrubbing won't dislodge it, and trying only risks wearing away enamel and injuring the gums.

Removing tartar requires instruments made for the job. Hand scalers and ultrasonic scalers can fracture the mineral bond and lift the deposit off cleanly, under the control of a trained clinician who can see what's healthy tooth and what isn't. That precision is exactly why it's a professional task rather than a home one.

Where tartar likes to hide

Tartar tends to collect fastest near the openings of the salivary glands, because that's where the mineral supply is richest. Two areas are especially prone:

  • Behind the lower front teeth. Salivary glands under the tongue sit right here, so the inner surfaces of the bottom front teeth often build up tartar first.
  • The cheek side of the upper back molars. Glands near the upper molars make this another common site.

Tartar can also form below the gumline, where it's out of sight entirely. This hidden calculus is particularly relevant to gum health, because it keeps bacteria lodged against the gum tissue and fuels inflammation.

Why it matters beyond the rough feeling

Tartar isn't just unsightly or coarse to the tongue. Its porous surface shelters bacteria and holds them against the teeth and gums, which contributes to gum inflammation, bleeding and, over time, gum disease. Removing it isn't cosmetic housekeeping — it's part of protecting the tissues that hold your teeth in place.

The practical takeaway is reassuring: good daily habits and a regular schedule of professional cleaning work together, each doing what the other can't. If build-up has had time to accumulate, booking a dental hygiene visit is the appropriate way to clear it and reset the surface so your home routine can keep it at bay.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between plaque and tartar?

Plaque is a soft, sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth daily and can be brushed away. Tartar (calculus) is plaque that has hardened by taking up minerals from saliva; once hardened, it bonds firmly to the tooth and a toothbrush can no longer remove it.

How quickly does plaque turn into tartar?

Plaque can begin mineralising into tartar within a day or two if it isn't removed, which is why consistent daily brushing and flossing matters — it clears plaque during the window before it hardens.

Can I remove tartar at home?

No. Once plaque has hardened into tartar it bonds to the tooth surface and needs professional scaling to remove it safely. Attempting to pick it off at home risks injuring the gums and tooth. A dental hygiene visit is the appropriate way to remove it.
