Oral Health After 65: Keeping Natural Teeth for Life

More Canadians than ever are keeping their natural teeth into their eighties and beyond. Doing so comfortably means understanding how the mouth changes with age — and adjusting care to match.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 8 min read

Losing teeth is not an inevitable part of getting older. With steady care, many people keep most of their natural teeth for life. But the mouth at 70 is not the mouth at 30 — and the care that keeps teeth healthy has to change along with it.

The shifts that come with age are usually gradual and often painless, which is exactly why they're easy to miss. Knowing what to watch for turns them from quiet problems into manageable ones.

Dry mouth: the side effect that quietly raises risk

One of the most common changes after 65 isn't caused by age itself but by the medications that often come with it. A great many prescriptions — for blood pressure, mood, allergies, bladder control and more — reduce saliva as a side effect. The more medications someone takes, the more pronounced the effect tends to be.

That matters because saliva does real work: it washes away food, neutralizes acid and helps protect enamel. A persistently dry mouth feels uncomfortable, makes chewing and speaking harder, and meaningfully raises the risk of decay. If your mouth feels dry much of the time, it's worth telling your dentist and your physician — there are ways to manage it, from sipping water and sugar-free lozenges to adjusting an offending medication where possible.

Worth Knowing

Dry mouth isn't just an annoyance — it changes the chemistry that protects your teeth. If you've started a new medication and noticed dryness, mention it at your next dental visit rather than waiting for a problem to show up.

Root decay and receding gums

Over decades, gums tend to recede, exposing the root of the tooth. Unlike the hard enamel crown, the root surface is softer and decays more readily. Combine that exposed surface with reduced saliva, and root decay becomes one of the more common dental problems in older adults.

It's manageable — fluoride, good daily cleaning, and regular professional care all help — but it's a strong argument against skipping checkups just because nothing hurts. Root decay often causes no pain until it's advanced.

Receding gums bring a second issue, too: sensitivity. Exposed roots can make hot, cold or sweet foods briefly uncomfortable. That's usually more of a nuisance than a danger, and a dentist can suggest ways to manage it — but it's worth mentioning rather than silently steering around cold water and ice cream for years.

Gum disease doesn't retire

Beyond recession, the gums themselves need ongoing attention. Periodontal (gum) disease tends to be a slow, quiet process, and years of accumulated effects can show up more noticeably in later life as looser teeth, persistent bad breath, or gums that bleed easily. Because it's largely painless until late, it's another condition that regular professional cleanings and examinations are designed to catch — and one that's far easier to stabilize early than to reverse once advanced.

Worn teeth and old dental work

A lifetime of chewing leaves its mark. Teeth wear down, edges chip, and the fillings and crowns placed years ago eventually reach the end of their service life. Old restorations can leak or fracture, and grinding (which can increase with certain medications or stress) accelerates wear. None of this is cause for alarm, but it does mean that teeth which have been trouble-free for decades still benefit from periodic review.

When brushing itself gets harder

Good daily cleaning is the foundation of keeping natural teeth — but arthritis, reduced grip strength or tremor can make a standard toothbrush awkward to use well. The answer is rarely to give up; it's to adapt:

  • An electric toothbrush has a larger handle and does much of the motion for you.
  • Building up a manual handle — with a foam grip, a bicycle grip, or even a tennis ball pushed onto it — makes it easier to hold.
  • Floss holders or interdental brushes can replace string floss when dexterity is limited.

A dentist or hygienist can suggest the right aids for your hands and recommend a technique that actually gets the job done.

A caregiver can play a real part here, too. If you're helping an older parent or partner, keeping their cleaning routine going — and noticing when it starts to slip — is one of the more valuable, low-effort things you can do for their health. A quiet decline in oral care often tracks with other changes worth watching.

The mouth-and-body connection

Oral health in later life isn't isolated from general health. Gum disease has established links with conditions such as diabetes and heart disease, and difficulty chewing can quietly narrow the diet toward soft, less nutritious foods. There's also the matter of oral cancer, the risk of which rises with age — another reason the screening built into a routine dental exam matters.

The nutrition point deserves a moment. When chewing becomes uncomfortable — from a sore tooth, an ill-fitting denture, or sensitivity — people naturally gravitate to softer, easier foods. Over months, that can mean fewer crunchy vegetables, less fibre and lower-quality nutrition, which has knock-on effects far beyond the mouth. Keeping teeth and any appliances comfortable isn't just about the mouth; it's about being able to eat a full, varied diet.

Medication is another thread that ties the mouth to the rest of the body. Because so many prescriptions affect saliva, bleeding or gum tissue, it's genuinely useful to keep your dental team updated on what you're taking and any changes. A current medication list helps them anticipate problems and plan treatment safely.

The simplest advice: don't stop going

The single most useful thing an older adult can do is keep up regular dental care rather than letting it lapse once the children's braces are paid off and life gets busy with other health appointments. Continued, consistent care is what catches the quiet changes early.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my mouth feel dry now that I take several medications?

Many common medications reduce saliva. Because saliva protects teeth and keeps tissues comfortable, a persistently dry mouth raises decay risk. Mention it to your dentist, who can suggest ways to manage it.

Is tooth decay still a concern after 65?

Yes — especially root decay. As gums recede, the softer root surface is exposed and decays more easily. It's a common problem in older adults and a key reason to keep up regular care.

Do I still need checkups if my teeth feel fine?

Yes. Many age-related changes are painless until advanced. Regular exams and cleanings catch dry-mouth effects, gum changes and early decay while they're easier to manage — and include oral cancer screening.
