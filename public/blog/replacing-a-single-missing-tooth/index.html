Replacing a Single Missing Tooth: Your Realistic Options

One gap can feel easy to ignore — especially out of sight at the back. But the teeth around it rarely leave it alone, which is why the question isn't only whether to replace it, but how.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

When a single tooth is missing, the realistic options come down to four: a dental implant, a conventional fixed bridge, a resin-bonded (conservative) bridge, or a removable partial denture. Each fills the gap, but they differ in what they ask of the surrounding teeth, how long they tend to last, and what they cost. The right one depends on your particular mouth — not a blanket rule.

Before comparing them, it's worth understanding why leaving the gap untouched isn't always the no-cost choice it appears to be.

What happens if you leave the gap

A single missing tooth sets off a few quiet changes. Teeth aren't fixed in place for life — they rely on their neighbours and their opposite number to stay put. Remove one and several things can follow:

  • Drifting: the teeth beside the gap can gradually tilt or slide into the empty space.
  • Over-eruption: the tooth that used to bite against the missing one can grow longer, having nothing to meet.
  • Bite changes: as teeth move, the way your teeth meet can shift, sometimes causing uneven wear or jaw discomfort.
  • Bone loss: the jawbone beneath the gap, no longer stimulated by a root, tends to shrink over time.

None of this is a reason to panic, and not every gap needs immediate action — a dentist can advise whether a particular missing tooth should be replaced and how soon. But understanding these changes explains why replacement is so often recommended.

Worth Knowing

Drifting and over-erupting teeth can make a later replacement more complicated, because the space and the bite have changed. That's one reason it's worth discussing options sooner rather than waiting until a problem forces the issue.

The four main options

Dental implant

A titanium post is placed in the jaw and topped with a crown. It stands alone without touching the neighbouring teeth, and because it replaces the root it helps preserve the bone. It needs adequate bone, reasonable general health, and a healing period, and it's typically the higher upfront cost. For a single gap with healthy neighbours, it's often the option that leaves the rest of the mouth most intact.

Conventional fixed bridge

The replacement tooth is suspended between two crowns cemented onto the adjacent teeth, which must be filed down to receive them. It avoids surgery and is usually completed faster than an implant. The trade-off is involving two neighbouring teeth — sensible if they already need crowns, less appealing if they're healthy.

Resin-bonded bridge

A more conservative bridge, where the replacement tooth is held by thin wings bonded to the back of an adjacent tooth with little or no filing. It preserves more natural tooth structure and often suits front teeth or specific situations. It isn't right for every gap, particularly under heavy biting forces, and your dentist will judge whether it's suitable.

Removable partial denture

A removable appliance with a false tooth that clips to the remaining teeth. It's the least expensive option and needs no surgery or tooth preparation, which makes it accessible or useful as a temporary measure. It's less stable than the fixed options and is taken out for cleaning, so many people view it as a stepping stone rather than a long-term solution.

How a dentist helps you choose

The decision isn't made from a list of pros and cons alone. A dentist examines the gap and the teeth around it, checks the bite, and usually takes imaging — often including a CBCT scan if an implant is being considered — to assess the bone. From there, a few questions guide the choice:

  • Are the neighbouring teeth healthy, or do they already need work?
  • Is there enough bone for an implant, or would grafting be needed first?
  • What are your general health, timeline and budget?
  • Is the gap at the front, where appearance matters, or the back, where chewing force dominates?

The best option is the one whose trade-offs line up with your situation and priorities. That's a conversation to have with a clinician who has seen your X-rays — the goal of this guide is simply to help you arrive at it already knowing the terrain.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a problem to leave one missing tooth?

It can be. Neighbouring teeth may drift into the gap, the tooth opposite can over-erupt, the bite can change, and bone beneath the gap tends to shrink. A dentist can advise whether and how soon a particular gap should be addressed.

What is a resin-bonded bridge?

It's a conservative bridge where the replacement tooth is held by thin wings bonded to the back of an adjacent tooth, with little or no filing. It suits certain situations — often front teeth — but isn't right for every gap.

Which option is best?

There's no single best option for everyone. The right choice depends on the health of the neighbouring teeth, the bone, your general health, budget and preference. A dentist weighs these together after an examination and imaging.
