Tooth Extraction and Oral Surgery: What to Expect and How to Recover

Removing a tooth is routine, but a smooth recovery depends on a handful of simple habits in the first few days. Here's what happens, and how to help yourself heal.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

Most tooth extractions are quick, well-controlled procedures done under local anaesthetic, and most people recover within a few days. The biggest factor you control is aftercare — protecting the blood clot that forms in the socket, keeping the area clean, and managing swelling. Knowing the steps in advance takes much of the worry out of it.

Extractions and minor oral surgery are among the most common procedures in dentistry. They're recommended when a tooth can't be saved, is severely damaged or infected, is badly impacted, or needs to be removed to make way for other treatment. Here's a plain walk-through of what's involved and how recovery usually goes.

Simple versus surgical extraction

There are two broad approaches, and which one applies depends on the tooth:

  • Simple extraction. For a tooth that is fully erupted and accessible, the tooth is loosened and lifted out with instruments. It's usually straightforward and done under local anaesthetic.
  • Surgical extraction. For a tooth that is impacted, broken at the gum line, or has awkward roots, the procedure may involve a small incision in the gum, and sometimes removing a little bone or sectioning the tooth to ease it out. Stitches may be placed. It's still a common procedure, just a bit more involved.

Anaesthesia and sedation options

Comfort during the procedure is a planned part of it. Options range from the straightforward to the more involved:

  • Local anaesthetic numbs the specific area and is standard for most extractions. You're awake but shouldn't feel pain.
  • Sedation — such as inhaled or oral sedation — may be offered for anxious patients or longer procedures, to help you feel relaxed and detached while still responsive.
  • Deeper sedation or general anaesthesia is reserved for more complex surgery or particular medical circumstances, and involves additional planning.

Which is appropriate depends on the procedure, your health and your preferences. Your clinician will discuss the options, and if you're sedated you'll need someone to take you home.

The Clot Is Everything

After an extraction, a blood clot forms in the socket. That clot is the foundation for healing — it protects the bone and nerves underneath and is where new tissue grows from. Most aftercare advice exists to protect it. Disturbing it early is the main cause of complications.

Aftercare: protecting the clot and avoiding dry socket

The first 24 hours matter most. These steps lower the chance of dry socket — a painful condition where the clot is lost and the socket is left exposed:

  • Bite on gauze with gentle, steady pressure for the time your clinician advises to settle any bleeding.
  • Rest and keep your head slightly elevated for the first day; avoid strenuous activity.
  • Don't rinse vigorously, spit forcefully, or use a straw for the first day or so — the suction and swishing can dislodge the clot.
  • Don't smoke or vape; it meaningfully raises the risk of dry socket and slows healing.
  • After the first day, rinse gently with warm salt water a few times a day to keep the area clean, especially after meals.
  • Keep brushing the rest of your mouth, staying gentle near the site.

Managing swelling, diet and pain

Some swelling and soreness are normal. A cold compress on the outside of the cheek for the first day helps limit swelling; after that, warmth can be soothing. Stick to soft, cool or lukewarm foods at first — think yogurt, eggs, soup that isn't hot, mashed vegetables — and ease back to a normal diet as comfort allows. Over-the-counter pain relief taken as directed usually manages discomfort well; follow any specific guidance your clinician gives, including for prescribed medication.

A few practical tips make those first days easier. Avoid very hot drinks, crunchy or crumbly foods, and anything with small seeds or grains that can lodge in the socket. Stay well hydrated, but drink from the glass rather than through a straw. If you were given an antibiotic or other medication, finish it as directed rather than stopping once you feel better. And give yourself permission to rest — trying to power through a normal day often prolongs the soreness rather than shortening it.

Preparing for the appointment

A little planning makes the day go more smoothly. Share your full medical history and a current list of medications with the clinic beforehand — blood thinners, certain bone medications and some health conditions can affect how a procedure is planned, so this isn't a formality. Ask whether you should eat beforehand; this depends on the type of anaesthesia, and sedation often has its own instructions. If you're being sedated, arrange a ride home and someone to stay with you for a few hours. It's also sensible to pick up soft foods and any recommended pain relief in advance, so you're not shopping while you'd rather be resting.

A rough recovery timeline

Everyone heals at their own pace, but a typical pattern looks like this:

  • Day 1: minor bleeding settles; swelling and soreness begin. Rest and protect the clot.
  • Days 2–3: swelling often peaks then starts to ease; gentle salt-water rinses begin.
  • Days 3–5: most people feel noticeably better and return to normal routines.
  • Week 1–2: the gum closes over the socket; any stitches dissolve or are removed.
  • Weeks to months: the bone underneath continues remodelling beneath the healed gum.

Surgical extractions generally take a little longer at each stage than simple ones.

Warning signs — when to call

Reach out to your dental office if you notice bleeding that won't stop with pressure, pain that gets worse after the first few days instead of improving (a possible sign of dry socket), increasing swelling, fever, pus or a persistent bad taste, or numbness that doesn't fade as the anaesthetic should. Difficulty breathing or swallowing is a medical emergency — seek urgent care. A clinic that provides oral surgery will give you tailored aftercare instructions and a number to call if something doesn't feel right.

It's worth remembering that calling with a question is never an imposition — clinics expect it, and catching a problem like dry socket early makes it far simpler to manage. If you're ever unsure whether what you're feeling is normal healing or a sign that something is off, the safest move is to ask rather than wait and see. Most recoveries are uneventful, and a brief check-in is a small price for peace of mind.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dry socket, and how do I avoid it?

Dry socket occurs when the protective clot is dislodged or fails to form, exposing bone and nerves and causing throbbing pain a few days after extraction. Lower the risk by not smoking, avoiding straws and vigorous rinsing early on, and following your aftercare instructions. If you suspect it, call your dentist.

How long does recovery take?

The gum usually closes within a couple of weeks and soft tissue heals over a few weeks, while the bone remodels for months underneath. Most people feel much better within three to five days. Surgical extractions take somewhat longer than simple ones.

When should I call after surgery?

Call for bleeding that won't stop, pain that worsens after a few days, increasing swelling, fever, pus or a bad taste, or numbness that doesn't resolve. Difficulty breathing or swallowing needs urgent medical care right away.
