What to Expect at Your First Visit with a New Dental Office

A first appointment at a new practice is less about treatment than about getting the full picture. Knowing the sequence ahead of time takes most of the nerves out of it.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

A good first visit with a new dental office is a bit like a thorough physical: the team isn't just looking for the one thing that's bothering you, they're building a baseline of your whole mouth. That's why it usually takes longer than a routine check-up and covers more ground. Here's what each part of it is for.

None of this should feel mysterious. If you know the steps, you can also tell whether a practice is being thorough — or cutting corners.

Before you arrive: paperwork and history

Expect to complete forms covering your contact details, insurance or CDCP information, and — importantly — your medical history. That last part isn't a formality. Conditions like heart disease, diabetes or pregnancy, and medications such as blood thinners, all influence how dental care is delivered. Many offices now send these forms ahead of time so you can fill them in at home.

The conversation first

A thorough dentist usually begins by talking, not drilling. They'll ask what's brought you in, whether anything specific is bothering you, how you feel about dental visits generally, and what your goals are. This is your moment to flag anxiety, sensitivity, or a history of difficult appointments — a good team will adjust its pace accordingly.

The clinical examination

The hands-on exam is the heart of the visit. It typically includes several distinct checks:

  • Tooth-by-tooth assessment — looking for decay, cracks, worn fillings and other issues on each tooth.
  • Periodontal (gum) charting — measuring the small spaces between your gums and teeth to gauge gum health and catch early gum disease. It's quick and routine.
  • Oral cancer screening — a check of the soft tissues of your mouth, tongue, cheeks and throat. It takes moments and is an important part of a complete exam.
  • Bite and jaw check — how your teeth come together, and whether there are signs of grinding or jaw-joint strain.
Worth Knowing

The oral cancer screening is one of the quietly valuable parts of a dental visit — it's painless, takes under a minute, and is one of the main reasons regular check-ups matter even when nothing hurts.

X-rays — but only if needed

Radiographs let the dentist see between and below teeth, where the eye can't reach. If you have recent X-rays from a previous dentist, ask to have them transferred — a good office won't duplicate imaging unnecessarily. New X-rays are taken when none are available or the existing ones are too old to be useful.

The cleaning

Many first visits include a cleaning (scaling and polishing) with the hygienist, though some offices complete the exam first and book the cleaning separately — particularly if your gums need more involved attention. If a cleaning matters to you at the first appointment, simply ask when you book.

Your findings, plan and estimate

At the end, the dentist should walk you through what they found, ideally showing you on the X-rays or intraoral photos. If treatment is recommended, you're entitled to understand why, what the alternatives are (including watchful waiting where appropriate), and what it will cost. A clear written estimate before any work begins is a reasonable expectation. What is and isn't needed depends on your individual examination — no honest plan is decided before the dentist has actually looked.

How to make the visit go smoothly

  • Bring a list of your medications and any medical conditions.
  • Arrange for records and recent X-rays to transfer in advance.
  • Bring your insurance or CDCP details.
  • Write down any questions or concerns beforehand — it's easy to forget them in the chair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will I get a cleaning at my first visit?

Often, but not always. Some offices complete the exam and assessment first and schedule the cleaning separately, especially when more involved work is needed. Ask when you book so you know what to expect.

Do I need X-rays as a new patient?

Not if you have recent images that can be transferred from a previous dentist. New radiographs are taken only when none are available or the existing ones are out of date.

What is periodontal charting?

It's a measurement of the small spaces between your gums and teeth to assess gum health. It's quick, routine, and helps the dentist spot early gum disease.
