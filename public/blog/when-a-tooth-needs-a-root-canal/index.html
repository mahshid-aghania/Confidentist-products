When a Tooth Might Need a Root Canal — and What the Treatment Involves

A root canal has a worse reputation than it deserves. For a tooth whose nerve is inflamed or infected, it's the treatment that ends the pain and lets you keep the tooth.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 8 min read

A root canal treats the inside of a tooth, not the surface. When the soft tissue at the core of a tooth — the pulp — becomes inflamed or infected, cleaning out that tissue and sealing the space can save a tooth that would otherwise be lost. Despite its reputation, the procedure is what relieves the pain, not what causes it.

If a dentist has mentioned the possibility, it helps to understand what's actually going on inside the tooth and what the appointment involves. None of what follows is a diagnosis — only an examination and X-rays can tell you whether a specific tooth needs treatment — but knowing the basics makes the conversation easier.

What the pulp is, and why it matters

Beneath the hard outer enamel and the layer of dentin underneath sits the pulp: a small chamber of soft tissue running down through the roots. It contains the tooth's nerve and blood supply. In a developing tooth the pulp helps it grow; in a mature tooth it mostly provides sensation.

A tooth can function perfectly well after the pulp is removed, because the surrounding bone and ligament keep it anchored and nourished. That's the principle behind endodontic — meaning "inside the tooth" — treatment. The problem is what happens when the pulp is damaged and left untreated: inflammation builds, bacteria can take hold, and infection can spread into the bone around the root.

Signs a tooth may be involved

Pulp trouble can announce itself loudly or creep up quietly. Some teeth ache severely; others feel almost normal until an X-ray reveals a problem. Patterns a dentist looks for include:

  • Lingering sensitivity to hot or cold. A twinge that fades in a second or two is usually ordinary. Pain that lingers for many seconds or minutes after the stimulus is gone is more concerning.
  • Pain when biting or applying pressure. A tooth that hurts specifically when you chew on it may have inflammation at the root tip.
  • Swelling or a tender gum. A small bump on the gum, facial swelling, or a pimple-like spot that drains can point to infection.
  • Discolouration. A tooth that darkens compared with its neighbours may have a pulp that has died, often after an old injury.
  • Spontaneous or throbbing pain, including pain that wakes you at night.

Deep decay, a crack, repeated dental work on the same tooth, or a past knock to the mouth are common routes to pulp damage. Because these signs overlap with other dental problems, they're a reason to be seen — not a self-diagnosis.

Worth Knowing

A tooth can need treatment without hurting. If the nerve has already died, pain may disappear even as infection quietly continues around the root. That's one reason regular check-ups and X-rays catch problems that symptoms alone would miss.

The modern procedure, step by step

Today's endodontic treatment, often done with magnification and rotary instruments, is closer to a routine filling than the ordeal of popular imagination. A typical visit looks like this:

  1. Numbing. Local anaesthetic is given so the tooth and surrounding area are fully numb. You'll feel pressure and movement, but not pain.
  2. Isolating the tooth. A thin rubber sheet (a dental dam) is placed to keep the tooth clean, dry and protected during treatment.
  3. Accessing the pulp. A small opening is made through the top of the tooth to reach the pulp chamber and the root canals.
  4. Cleaning and shaping. The inflamed or infected tissue is removed, and the narrow canals are cleaned, disinfected and shaped.
  5. Sealing. The canals are filled with a rubber-like material and sealed, and a temporary or permanent filling closes the access opening.

Treatment may be completed in one or two visits depending on the tooth and the degree of infection. Afterward the tooth is usually tender for a few days, which over-the-counter pain relief generally manages well.

Why it relieves pain rather than causes it

The severe pain people dread is almost always the pre-treatment problem: an inflamed, pressurised pulp with nowhere to drain. By removing that tissue and clearing the infection, the procedure takes away the source. The soreness that follows is ordinary healing, not the return of the original ache.

The crown that usually follows

Saving the pulp is only half the job; the tooth still has to survive years of chewing. A back tooth in particular can become brittle after treatment and is prone to fracturing. For that reason a dentist often recommends a crown — a custom cap that covers and strengthens the tooth. Front teeth sometimes need only a smaller restoration. Which is right depends on how much healthy tooth remains, something your dentist assesses case by case.

The timing matters too. The access opening is usually closed with a temporary or permanent filling at the end of treatment, and the permanent crown may be fitted at a follow-up visit once the tooth has settled. Leaving a treated back tooth without its final restoration for too long increases the risk that it cracks, so it's worth asking at the outset what the full plan looks like and roughly when each step happens.

How a tooth is monitored afterward

A root canal isn't quite the end of the story. Because the tooth no longer has a live nerve, it won't warn you with sensitivity if something changes, so periodic check-ups and the occasional X-ray are how a dentist confirms that the area around the root is healing as expected. Most treated teeth go on to function normally for many years; occasionally a tooth needs re-treatment or further assessment if symptoms return. Keeping your regular recall appointments is the simplest way to catch any issue early, and it costs nothing in discomfort to do so.

What to do if you suspect a problem

Lingering pain, swelling or a darkening tooth is worth a prompt call to a dentist. In the meantime, over-the-counter pain relief used as directed and avoiding very hot or cold foods on that side can help — but these ease symptoms, they don't treat the cause. Never place aspirin or any tablet directly against the gum or tooth; it doesn't help and can burn the tissue. If you have facial swelling, fever or difficulty swallowing, treat it as urgent and seek care right away. A practice that offers root canal treatment can assess the tooth, confirm whether the pulp is involved, and explain your options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a root canal painful?

The tooth is numbed with local anaesthetic, so you shouldn't feel pain during treatment. Most of the discomfort people link to root canals comes from the inflamed pulp beforehand — the procedure relieves it. Mild tenderness for a few days afterward is normal and usually eases with over-the-counter pain relief.

Could the tooth just be pulled instead?

Sometimes, but keeping a natural tooth is generally preferable where it can be saved. A gap left by an extraction may later need a bridge or implant to restore chewing. Your dentist can walk you through the trade-offs for your particular tooth.

Why is a crown recommended afterward?

A treated tooth, especially a molar, can become brittle and more likely to crack. A crown covers and protects it so it can handle everyday chewing. Not every tooth needs one — your dentist will advise based on how much sound tooth structure remains.
