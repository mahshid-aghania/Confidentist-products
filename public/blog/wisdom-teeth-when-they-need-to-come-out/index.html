Wisdom Teeth: When They Need to Come Out (and When They Don't)

Removal isn't automatic. Some wisdom teeth cause genuine trouble; others sit quietly for a lifetime. The right call comes down to what an examination actually shows.

Reviewed by Dr. Mehdi Adibrad, Dental Surgeon • 7 min read

Not everyone needs their wisdom teeth out. A wisdom tooth that comes in fully, sits in a useful position, and can be kept clean can often stay put. Removal is considered when a tooth is causing problems — or is likely to — because of impaction, infection, decay or crowding. The decision rests on an individual examination and X-rays, not on a blanket rule.

Wisdom teeth, the third molars at the back of each jaw, usually appear in the late teens or early twenties. By then the rest of the mouth is set, and there isn't always room for them. That's why they get so much attention — and why the question is really about whether a particular tooth is helping, harmless, or heading for trouble.

What impaction means

A tooth is "impacted" when it can't fully emerge into a normal position, usually for lack of space or because it's blocked by the tooth in front, the jawbone or gum. Impaction isn't all-or-nothing; clinicians describe it by how the tooth is angled:

  • Mesial impaction — the tooth tilts forward toward the molar in front. This is the most common pattern.
  • Vertical impaction — the tooth is upright but hasn't fully erupted.
  • Horizontal impaction — the tooth lies on its side, often pressing against its neighbour.
  • Distal impaction — the tooth tilts backward, away from the other molars.

A partially erupted tooth can leave a flap of gum that traps food and bacteria, which is a frequent source of problems. A fully buried tooth may cause nothing at all, or may occasionally be linked to issues in the surrounding bone.

Symptoms that prompt a closer look

Wisdom teeth don't always hurt, but certain signs are worth a dental assessment:

  • Aching or pressure at the back of the jaw.
  • Red, swollen or tender gum over a partly erupted tooth (a condition called pericoronitis).
  • Repeated infections, bad taste, or difficulty opening the mouth fully.
  • Decay in the wisdom tooth or the molar next to it, often because the area is hard to clean.
  • Pain or crowding that seems to involve the surrounding teeth.

Reasons to remove — and reasons to monitor

The honest answer is that it depends. Common reasons a surgeon recommends removal include recurrent infection around a partly erupted tooth, decay or gum disease that can't be managed, damage to the neighbouring molar, or a cyst forming around an impacted tooth. On the other hand, a healthy, fully erupted, functional wisdom tooth that you can brush and floss has no automatic reason to go.

A Balanced View

Where a wisdom tooth is causing no symptoms and no detectable problem, careful monitoring is a legitimate option. Many such teeth are kept and reviewed over time. Extraction is a decision for situations where the benefits of removing outweigh leaving it in place — judged case by case.

Why timing can matter

When removal is indicated, it is often more straightforward earlier in adulthood. In younger patients the roots are typically less fully formed and the bone is more pliable, which can translate into a more predictable procedure and easier recovery. This is a general tendency rather than a reason to remove healthy teeth — and plenty of people have uneventful extractions later in life. Your clinician will weigh your age alongside everything else.

What an assessment involves

Deciding anything sensible requires seeing the full picture. A thorough evaluation usually includes a clinical examination of how the teeth sit and function, a review of your symptoms and medical history, and imaging — commonly a panoramic X-ray — to show the position of each wisdom tooth and its relationship to important structures such as the nerve in the lower jaw and the sinus above the upper teeth. That imaging helps the surgeon explain the likely difficulty, the options, and any particular considerations for your case.

Why they cause trouble so often

Wisdom teeth are, in a sense, running out of room. Over a long span of human history jaws have tended to be smaller than the full set of thirty-two teeth strictly needs, and the third molars are last to arrive — after every other tooth has claimed its space. When there's nowhere comfortable for them to sit, they emerge at an angle, only partway, or not at all. Their position right at the back of the mouth adds a second difficulty: even a wisdom tooth that comes in reasonably well can be hard to reach with a toothbrush, which makes it more prone to decay and gum inflammation than teeth further forward. Those two factors — limited space and poor access for cleaning — explain most of the problems that bring wisdom teeth to attention.

If you decide to keep them

Choosing to keep a wisdom tooth isn't a passive decision; it comes with a little extra care. Because the back of the mouth is awkward to clean, it's worth paying particular attention to brushing the area thoroughly and using floss or an interdental brush to clear the gum line around the tooth and its neighbour. Mentioning your wisdom teeth at routine check-ups lets your dentist keep an eye on them over time, watching for early decay, gum pocketing or any change in position. If a previously quiet tooth starts giving trouble later — recurrent soreness, swelling, or a bad taste around it — that's the moment to revisit the question rather than wait it out.

Talking it through

If a dentist has flagged your wisdom teeth, a focused conversation helps: is this tooth causing a problem now, is it likely to, and what happens if we wait? A balanced assessment should present both removal and monitoring where each is reasonable. A clinic offering wisdom tooth removal can examine the teeth, review your X-rays, and explain which path fits your situation — and when doing nothing is the right choice.

However the conversation goes, the sensible aim is a decision that fits the evidence in front of you rather than a default in either direction. A healthy tooth that can be cleaned and reviewed deserves the benefit of the doubt; a tooth that is repeatedly infected, decayed or threatening its neighbour has earned a closer look at removal. Asking why a particular recommendation is being made, and what the alternatives are, is reasonable and usually welcomed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does everyone need their wisdom teeth out?

No. Wisdom teeth that are healthy, fully erupted, functional and cleanable can often stay. Removal is considered when they cause problems or carry a clear risk of future problems — a judgement based on your examination and X-rays.

What exactly is an impacted wisdom tooth?

It's a wisdom tooth that can't fully emerge into a normal position — usually for lack of space, or because it's angled against the tooth in front, the jawbone or gum. Impaction can be partial or complete, and it's a common reason removal is discussed.

Is it easier to have them removed when you're younger?

When removal is needed, it's often more straightforward in younger patients because the roots are less developed and the bone more pliable, which can mean smoother healing. That's a general pattern, not a reason to remove healthy teeth. Your clinician will advise on timing.
