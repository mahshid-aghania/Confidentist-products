Your Child's First Dental Visit: How to Prepare (and Why Timing Matters)

A first trip to the dentist sets the tone for how a child feels about dental care for years. A little preparation — and the right timing — makes a surprising difference.

Reviewed by Dr. Neda Kadivar, General & Family Dentist • 7 min read

Most parents assume the first dental visit can wait until there's a full set of teeth, or until a child is old enough to sit still. The Canadian Dental Association points the other way: a child should see a dentist within six months of the first tooth appearing, or by their first birthday — whichever comes first.

That timing can feel early. Your one-year-old has only a handful of teeth and no idea what's happening. But that's exactly the point. The first visit isn't really about fixing anything; it's about starting a relationship, spotting habits worth adjusting, and letting your child learn that the dentist is a calm, ordinary place.

Why "by age one" matters more than it sounds

Early visits do quiet, useful work. The dentist checks how the teeth and jaw are coming in, looks for early signs of decay (which can appear even on baby teeth), and talks with you about bottle habits, thumb-sucking, and how to clean those first teeth. None of this requires a cooperative patient — a lap exam on a parent's knee is often all it takes.

There's also a habit being formed. A child who meets the dentist before anything hurts tends to carry less fear into later visits. Waiting until there's a problem means the first memory of the dentist is tied to discomfort — a harder start to undo.

Early visits give the dentist a baseline, too. Seeing your child from the start means later changes are easier to spot, and it lets the dentist tailor advice to how your child's mouth is actually developing rather than offering generic guidance. If a habit like prolonged bottle use or thumb-sucking is heading toward a problem, the earlier it's flagged, the gentler the correction tends to be.

The one habit worth catching early

One specific concern dentists watch for in very young children is early childhood decay, sometimes linked to a baby falling asleep with a bottle of milk or juice. Liquid pooling around the teeth overnight feeds the bacteria that cause decay. It's an easy habit to adjust once you know about it — water in the bedtime bottle, or none at all — but far easier to prevent than to treat. An early visit is where this kind of practical, specific advice lands best.

Worth Knowing

Baby teeth matter. They hold space for adult teeth, help with speech and chewing, and can develop decay that causes real pain. Caring for them early isn't optional just because they'll eventually fall out.

What actually happens at the appointment

For a toddler, the visit is short — often 15 to 30 minutes. Expect the dentist to count the teeth, check the gums and bite, and look for anything unusual. Depending on your child's age and comfort, there may be a gentle cleaning or a fluoride application. You'll likely spend as much time talking as the dentist spends looking: feeding, cleaning routines, and what to watch for at home.

For a slightly older child who is seeing the dentist for the first time, the visit may include a fuller look and perhaps X-rays, but a good practice will still move at the child's pace. Nobody expects a perfect patient on day one.

How to talk about it at home

Children take their cues from you. If you treat the appointment as routine and positive, they're far more likely to arrive relaxed.

  • Keep your own language neutral. Avoid words like "hurt", "needle", "drill", or even reassurances like "it won't be scary" — which plant the idea that it might be. "The dentist is going to count your teeth" is plenty.
  • Read a picture book about visiting the dentist in the days beforehand, or play a quick pretend game where a stuffed animal "opens wide".
  • Time it well. Book a morning slot after a nap and a snack, not at the end of a long, overtired day.
  • Let them bring a comfort object — a favourite toy or blanket in the chair is completely fine.

What parents can do in the chair

Your job during the visit is to be a calm anchor, not a coach. You don't need to narrate or promise rewards for bravery. Sit close, hold your child if the dentist invites a lap exam, and let the dental team lead. If your child cries, that's expected — a short, gentle first visit that ends on a friendly note is a win, even if very little "got done".

It also helps to manage your own expectations quietly. A first appointment is not a report card. Some children open wide and love the ride in the chair; others spend the whole time deciding whether to trust the room. Both are perfectly normal, and neither predicts how the next visit will go. Resist the urge to apologize for your child or to push them past their limit — a positive ending matters more than a complete one, because it's the feeling they'll bring back next time.

A quick note on what the visit is checking for

Parents often wonder what, exactly, the dentist is looking at in a mouth with so few teeth. Beyond counting what's there, the dentist checks that teeth are erupting in a healthy pattern, looks at the bite and how the jaw is aligning, inspects the gums and soft tissues, and watches for any early white spots on the enamel that can signal the very beginning of decay. None of this takes long, and most of it the dentist can do while chatting with you — which is partly why keeping the mood light makes the exam easier to complete.

Setting up the years that follow

After the first visit, most children settle into checkups every six months, though your dentist may suggest a different interval based on their risk. Between visits, the daily basics matter most: brushing twice a day with an appropriate amount of fluoride toothpaste, helping until your child can clean thoroughly on their own (usually around age seven or eight), and keeping sugary drinks occasional rather than constant.

If you're choosing a practice for a young family, look for one that regularly sees children and is unhurried with them. A clinic that offers children's first dental visit appointments in a child-friendly way can make those early years much easier — but the strongest predictor of a confident young patient is a parent who stays relaxed and starts early.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should my child have their first dental visit?

Within six months of the first tooth appearing, or by one year of age — whichever comes first, per the Canadian Dental Association. Early visits are brief and focus on familiarity rather than treatment.

Isn't my baby too young for a real appointment?

No. An early visit lets the dentist check how teeth and gums are developing, review feeding and cleaning habits, and catch concerns before they become painful — all while your child is still relaxed.

What if my child cries or won't cooperate?

That's normal, and dental teams plan for it. A short visit that ends with a quick look and a friendly goodbye still counts. At this age the goal is comfort, not a complete exam.
